\begin{abstract}
The definition of accessibility encompasses the role of the
attractiveness of opportunities at potential destinations that people
consider valuable. This study revises the common assumption in empirical
studies that residents are equally attracted to all types of employment
and examines its implications for public transport evaluation from a
social equity perspective. Additionally, the role of the modifiable
areal unit problem (MAUP) is also explored in this relationship. The
study draws on the case of Greater Mexico City over a ten-year period,
in which seven temporal stages of the main public transport network are
examined. The key results highlight a significant difference between
accessibility measures that account for employment matching and those
that do not, though these distinctions diminish when lower spatial
resolutions are used. The spatial analysis also shows that the
differences are consistently larger for lower-educated populations. In
terms of public transport infrastructure evaluation over time, the study
confirms that relying on simple measures, such as the global average,
may overlook critical transport equity insights. Additionally, the
impact of including employment matching in equity analyses varies, with
outcomes differing case by case. Depending on the accessibility measure,
the analyses show that a transport improvement might have progressive
effects with one measure, while another measure may indicate regressive
effects, or both measures can sometimes align. Overall, the comparisons
between measures suggest their complementarity in equity evaluations.
The findings have implications for policy analysts, given the systematic
differences in how transport projects tend to affect less-educated
populations and the heterogeneity in the type of population impacted by
specific transport projects on a case-by-case basis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

A key function of a transport system is to provide people with access to
different locations at different times so that they can physically
participate in a diverse range of activities (Miller 2018).
Accessibility therefore has an important role to play in transport
planning, and various measures have been proposed (see Geurs and van Wee
(2023) for a summary). Location-based measures, which measure the level
of accessibility to a particular type of activity available at a
location, have been especially popular. They have been used to study the
impacts of accessibility on property prices (Osland and Thorsen 2008),
labour market outcomes (Johnson, Ercolani, and Mackie 2017), and social
equity (Tiznado-Aitken, Muñoz, and Hurtubia 2021), among other things.

A common application of the measures is to quantify accessibility to
employment. Measuring a location's access to jobs can incorporate a wide
range of factors. A simple measure might take into account only the
location of employment opportunities and a measure of impedance (e.g.,
travel time) incurred to reach those destinations from a given origin.
One assumption implicit in such a simple measure, and one common in
empirical studies, is that all types of employment are potentially
relevant for, and equally attractive to, everyone. Studies examining the
implications of relaxing this assumptions are rare. See Osland (2010) or
Thériault, Voisin, and Des Rosiers (2013) for examples.

This paper makes an original contribution by examining some of the
implications of relaxing the assumption of equally attractive employment
opportunities. Instead of this assumption, with a basis in Human Capital
Theory, we categorise workers and jobs according to education and income
level. Workers only match with a job if their level of education
corresponds to the job's level of income. We examine the implications of
this change on spatial patterns of accessibility and on accessibility
poverty calculations. Additional novelty is added by considering how the
modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) might impact on changes in the way
accessibility is calculated. Accordingly, we address three research
questions:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  What are the key spatial differences between generic and matching
  accessibility measures and what is the role of the MAUP?
\item
  How does the evaluation of PT infrastructure differs when accounting
  for matching employment opportunities in accessibility measures?
\item
  What are the social equity implications of the above?
\end{enumerate}

Our study is set in Greater Mexico City (GMC), an understudied
mega-city. We make use of multi-source spatial data to examine the
impacts of a number of expansions of the main public transport network
(MPTN) over the period 2009 to 2019. In general, we find that accounting
for heterogeneity among workers and jobs can have important implications
for the conclusions we draw about how changes to the transport network
alter patterns of accessibility and accessibility poverty. Our analysis
of MAUP showed that larger areal units tend to minimise the difference
in accessibility measures accounting for heterogeneity and those that
ignore it. However, zoning effects can lead to some of the differences
being maintained even when moving to larger units.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section
\ref{sec:lit_review} presents a review of the relevant literature.
Section \ref{sec:methods} outlines the data and methods used in the
study. Our findings are presented in Section \ref{sec:findings} and then
discussed in Section \ref{sec:discussion}. Lastly, some concluding
remarks are offered in Section \ref{sec:conclusions}.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Literature review
\label{sec:lit_review}}{Literature review }}\label{literature-review}

Levinson and Wu (2020) assert that accessibility measures ``the ease of
reaching valued destinations'' (p.~130). This notion combines two basic
elements, namely the ease of travel and the spatial dispersion of
opportunities reflected in land use patterns, including services and
other people, for example. Furthermore, this definition limits
opportunities to those which are deemed valuable. Thus, the \emph{for
whom} question becomes relevant. In line with this discussion,
Thériault, Voisin, and Des Rosiers (2013) provides a more explicit
definition of accessibility, regarding it as ``the ability that persons
living in that area can, within an acceptable travel time, reach a
satisfactory diversity of activity locations that are important to
them'' (p.~234).

The operationalisation of accessibility has taken various forms in the
literature. The most common are infrastructure-based, person-based,
place-based, and utility-based measures (Geurs and van Wee 2004).
Place-based measures are frequently used due to their consistency with
conceptual definitions, flexibility, and ease of interpretation,
enhancing their impact on transport policy. These measures characterise
space based on mobility constraints or enablers, combined with land use
patterns, by dividing regions into spatial units or zones. This review
focuses on location-based measures due to their relevance in policy
discussions and their ability to account for people's abilities and
preferences (Páez, Scott, and Morency 2012).

\subsection{Accessibility measures and empirical
application}\label{accessibility-measures-and-empirical-application}

Location-based measures consist of the sum of the product of two
functions, namely \(g(\cdot)\) and \(f(\cdot)\), as shown in Equation
\ref{eq:location-based1} (Páez, Scott, and Morency 2012):

\begin{equation}
   A^p_{ik} =  \sum_j g(W_{jk}) f(c^p_{ij}).
  \label{eq:location-based1}
\end{equation}

Here, \(A\) denotes the accessibility to opportunities of type \(k\)
from location \(i\), from the perspective of person type \(p\). The
measure reflects the attractiveness of opportunities \(W\) of type \(k\)
at destination \(j\). The term \(c_{ij}\) refers to a function of travel
cost between \(i\) and \(j\), as experienced by person \(p\).

Equation \ref{eq:location-based1} is often used in the literature to
measure accessibility to employment as follows:

\begin{equation}
  A_{i} = \sum^N_{i=1} E_j f(c_{ij})
  \label{eq:access-employment-general}
\end{equation}

The attractiveness of a destination \(W\) is expressed by \(E\), usually
as the number of employment opportunities at \(j\), i.e.~\(E_j\). Travel
cost in \(f(c_{ij})\) is often represented by travel time.

In Equation \ref{eq:access-employment-general}, households and
opportunities are implicitly assumed to be homogeneous, i.e., all types
of employment are equally attractive for all types of residents (Houston
2005; Shen 1998). However, contributions from theory highlight the
characteristics of the demand (residents at origins) and supply at
potential destinations (Geurs and van Wee 2004; Levinson and Wu 2020).
Specifically, Levinson and Wu state that ``different job categories
represent different economic activities, and different types of jobs
generally require different skills and education levels'' (2020, 147).
This distinction is supported by Human Capital Theory from labour market
studies (Schultz 1961; Becker 1994).

Location-based measures can consider some of these distinctive elements.
For instance, Equation \ref{eq:location-based1} refers to the following
components: (1) individual's profiles; (2) how different profiles
perceive or experience the space, and; (3) constraints that may prevent
segments of the population to access some type of activities. Some of
these aspects have been found to be relevant in empirical studies. For
instance, gender in Osland (2010) or age and gender in Thériault,
Voisin, and Des Rosiers (2013) in the study of the property prices.

The operationalisation of measures accounting for employment preferences
or restrictions can vary. In empirical studies, these characteristics
can be expressed as type of person \(m\) at \(j\) and type of employment
\(m\) at \(k\) (Pereira et al. 2019; Shen 1998; Pan, Jin, and Liu 2020).
Accordingly, only employment opportunities \(E\) matching the profile of
residents between \(j\) and \(k\) are considered in the accessibility
measure. This reflects the notion that residents would only find
attractive employment opportunities that match their skills and
education level.

In Pereira et al. (2019), an accessibility measure recognising such
characteristics is implemented as a \emph{proxy} match by distinguishing
the upper and lower halves of residents' income and education
requirements in employment opportunities. Specifically, the lower half
is matched only with employment opportunities requiring secondary or
primary education, while the upper half considers those requiring higher
or secondary education. Although the aim of this study is the
identification of potential inequalities in transport policy and not the
explicit discussion of this measure, the authors report no substantial
difference in their conclusions when disregarding the match in
accessibility measures.

Levinson and Wu (2020) suggest weighting employment in each category
with the number of eligible workers within each corresponding segment.
This perspective thus considers two aspects discussed in employment
mismatch literature, competition for employment opportunities and labour
segmentation. Bian (2020) refers to these aspects as a numerical
(objective) imbalance and an expectation imbalance (subjective),
respectively.

An empirical application which explores both numerical and expectation
imbalance can be found in Pan, Jin, and Liu (2020). To compute
accessibility measures in Houston, the study categorises employment
opportunities by the number of white- and blue-collar jobs at potential
destinations. On the residents' side, it considers unemployed
individuals as job seekers, categorising them by education level:
\emph{associate's degree or above} and \emph{high school degree or
lower}. White-collar jobs are relevant only to job seekers with an
associate's degree or higher, while blue-collar jobs are relevant to the
less educated group. The measures also account for different transport
modes, i.e., automobile, drive to transit, and walk to transit. Results
show that accessibility is concentrated in core areas, declining slowly
for drivers and much more sharply for transit users. The study concludes
that automobile are even more essential for low-educated groups since
blue-collar jobs are more dispersed across the city than white-collar
jobs. The study concludes low-educated groups are more dependent of car
since blue-collar jobs are more dispersed across the city than
white-collar jobs.

Pan, Jin, and Liu (2020) follow a `descriptive' rather than a
`prescriptive' approach (Páez, Scott, and Morency 2012). This limits its
use in transport policy evaluation, as it characterises multiple
residents within the same location rather than providing a single
measure for a particular zone. Additionally, it does not address the
differences or implications of using education and employment matching
compared to accessibility measures that disregard these distinctions.

\subsubsection{The modifiable areal unit problem in accessibility
measures}\label{the-modifiable-areal-unit-problem-in-accessibility-measures}

Location-based measures tend to characterise space in discrete units.
Thus, these often suffer from the MAUP. The MAUP literature
distinguishes two main types of issues, namely \emph{scale} effects and
\emph{zoning} effects (Wong 2009b). Scale effects refer to the
variability in spatial analysis results depending on the spatial
resolution used. Zoning effects, on the other hand, arise from the
spatial configuration of areas while maintaining the same number of
zones in an area of study. Unless location-based accessibility is
calculated at the individual level, it will likely be affected by these
issues. Therefore, this is an important consideration for most
location-based applications.

Considerable effort has been made to understand the effects and
structural patterns of the MAUP. The clearest effects are observed in
simple univariate statistics. It has been noted that while mean values
remain fairly constant across scales, variance tends to decline as
spatial resolution decreases, with a narrower range at lower resolutions
(Dark and Bram 2007; Wong 2009a). However, this structure tends to
dissipate in multivariate regression analyses (Fotheringham and Wong
1991). Zoning effects, on the other hand, are less predictable and are
influenced by factors such as spatial autocorrelation and aggregation
methods, e.g., mean, sum, median (Wong 2009a). Zoning effects are
minimal in cases of random spatial patterns, but results are highly
sensitive to zoning when strong positive spatial autocorrelation exists
(Lee et al. 2019).

Accessibility studies often use a single unit of analysis. Thus,
literature is limited in the role of MAUP in location-accessibility
(Stepniak and Rosik 2015). An empirical study in Poland examined three
gravity-type accessibility measures at different spatial scales. The
findings showed that larger units, such as municipalities, produced
smoother estimates compared to disaggregated grid-based inputs,
consistent with MAUP literature (Wong 2009a). Additionally, differences
in self-potential estimates were attributed to the gravity measures'
components (e.g., transport and land use) rather than the estimation
process itself. Further work has suggested that errors arise from
aggregating travel times within zones (intra-zone) and between zones
(inter-zone) (Stepniak and Jacobs-Crisioni 2017). A comparison with
disaggregated travel times suggests that using population-weighted
centroids provides more accurate results than geometric centroids or
areal-based methods Stepniak and Rosik (2015). Pereira, Grégoire, and
Karner (2019) investigated the equity effects of transport
infrastructure in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, taking MAUP into account. The
study conducted three analyses that utilised two hexagonal grids and
traffic zones. The findings indicate that while the direction of the
relationship between accessibility increments and residents' income
remained stable, there were significant differences in the magnitude and
significance of this association across different spatial aggregations.

Although there are recent and relevant contributions considering MAUP
and location-based accessibility measures, there are no studies
examining the interactions with refined of accessibility measures, e.g.,
measures considering matching opportunities.

\subsection{Equity evaluation of transport in transport
infrastructure}\label{equity-evaluation-of-transport-in-transport-infrastructure}

Transport equity concerns the adequate allocation of transport resources
among different population groups and places (Lucas et al. 2019). This
issue can be framed within the context of social justice, which is often
defined as the ``morally proper distribution of benefits and burdens
among members of a society'' (Lucas et al. 2019, 7). This definition
captures the essence of transport equity by incorporating three key
elements (ibid): 1) the resource being distributed, such as mobility or
accessibility; 2) the social attributes of society's members; and 3) the
moral principles used to assess whether a given allocation is fair or
equitable (Martens, Bastiaanssen, and Lucas 2019).

The `equity standard', implicit in the third point, is major topic of
debate in literature. The typical questions involve, for example, should
everyone receive the same amount of the good or carry the same burden,
should populations in most need receive the most, etc. Karner, Pereira,
and Farber (2024) discuss two main moral philosophies addressing these
questions, namely the egalitarian and the sufficientarian. The former
addresses the systematic exclusion or reduction of opportunities for
neglected populations. The latter is about ensuring that minimal
resources are allocated in a way that allows people to meet essential
needs.

Location-based accessibility measures have been used as a key benefit
being distributed in the evaluation of the performance of public
transport infrastructure in policy reports and research (Ermagun 2021;
Liu, Porr, and Miller 2022; Conway, Byrd, and Van Eggermond 2018;
Malekzadeh and Chung 2020; Palmateer et al. 2021). One motivation is
because the use of realistic travel times can reflect important elements
of a public transport system, such as the topology of the network,
spatial network, and operational characteristics.

In line with its philosophical and moral perspective, the
operationalisation of the accessibility poverty notion requires the
definition of a sufficiency threshold. Accessibility poverty draws
inspiration from the income poverty literature and adopts an analogous
poverty line (Martens and Bastiaanssen 2019). For analytical purposes,
studies often utilise a percentile of the number of accessible
opportunities. For example, Karner, Pereira, and Farber (2024) employs
the population-weighted median of job accessibility in the Washington
metropolitan area. Similarly, Martens and Bastiaanssen (2019) introduces
a combined index that incorporates the percentile of employment
accessible by car alongside income deprivation, indicating that an
individual or group is considered to be in accessibility poverty if they
face both low public transport accessibility and income deprivation.
This approach is founded on the understanding that marginalised
populations rely on public transport, and low-income households may
either be unable to afford the costs associated with maintaining a car
or find it undesirable to do so.

Despite the growing relevance of accessibility measures in the
evaluation of public transport infrastructure, knowledge of the
implications of overlooking matching employment are limited in the
literature. In this study, we aim to examine the implications of the use
of a matching accessibility measure accounting for the MAUP, and its
role in transport infrastructure equity evaluation. We cover a 10-year
period on a empirical study which includes a range of transport
projects.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Methods
\label{sec:methods}}{Methods }}\label{methods}

\subsection{Accessibility measures
examined}\label{accessibility-measures-examined}

For this study, a general accessibility measure, also referred to as
`All', is computed with the following equation:

\begin{equation}
  A^{All}_{jt} = \sum^J_{j=1} E_k f(c_{jkt})
   \label{eq:empirical-access-general}
\end{equation}

\(A^{All}_{jt}\) denotes the general accessibility to employment from
origin \(j\) at time \(t\). \(E_k\) are the employment opportunities at
potential destinations, and \(c^{PT}_{jkt}\) is the travel time from
\(j\) to \(k\) at temporal stage \(t\).

For this work, the implementation of the matching accessibility measure
is adapted from the literature according to the data available for the
area of study as follows (Pereira et al. 2019; Pan, Jin, and Liu 2020):

\begin{equation}
  A^{Matching}_{jtm} = \sum^J_{j=1} E_{km} f(c_{jkt}),
   \label{eq:empirical-access-matching}
\end{equation}

Here, \(A^{Matching}_{jtm}\) is the matching accessibility measure from
\(j\), at temporal stage \(t\), for opportunities of type \(m\). \(m\)
at \(j\) is categorised in education quintiles based on the median
number of years in formal education. \(m\) at \(k\) is based on the
average salary paid at destinations classified by quintiles as well.
Only employment opportunities \(E\) matching the same education quantile
between \(j\) and \(k\) are considered in this measure (when
\(j \neq k\)). This reflects that residents would deem valuable only
employment opportunities that match their education level. In our study
area, this is supported by the positive relationship between education
level and wages (Nuñez, Paredes, and Garduño-Rivera 2017; Rojas, Angulo,
and Velázquez 2000). An exception occurs when \(j = k\). In this case,
employment opportunities in the origin are considered as available. This
is to avoid the assumption implying that some zones have accessibility
values equal to 0. The specific data sources and procedures are detailed
in the next sub-sections.

\subsection{Study area}\label{study-area}

Greater Mexico City (GMC) (\emph{Zona Metropolitana del Valle de
México}) is located in the south-centre of Mexico. In 2015, GMC had
nearly 21 million inhabitants (INEGI 2015b). The official delimitation
consists of 76 adjacent municipalities, which are part of three
different states, namely: (1) \emph{Ciudad de México} (16
municipalities); (2) \emph{Hidalgo} (1 municipality), and (3)
\emph{México} (59 municipalities), as shown in Figure
\ref{fig:metro-map}. The total GMC area is 7,889 km\textsuperscript{2},
and it is home to 17\% of the national population.

\subsubsection{The main public transport network
(MPTN)}\label{the-main-public-transport-network-mptn}

The focus of this work is \emph{rapid transit} and \emph{semi-rapid
transit} infrastructure, excluding specialized modes i.e., \emph{aerial
tramway}. The combination of public transport modes included in the
analyses is referred to as the \emph{main public transport network}
(MPTN). Figure \ref{fig:metro-map} shows the MPTN in GMC in the year
2019. This consists of a variety of modes operated by the following main
agencies: The metro system, directly operated by the state-owned company
\emph{Sistema de Transporte Colectivo} (STC) and designated as Metro;
two BRT systems, namely \emph{Metrobus} (MB) in Ciudad de México and
Mexibus (MXB) in the state of México; One LRT line operated by STE; and
one regional rail line designated as \emph{Sub}.

The period of study was divided into seven temporal stages to reflect
significant changes in the MPTN over time. Initially, changes were
identified by the physical alterations in the MPTN, such as the opening
of a new corridor or the extension of an existing one. Table
\ref{tab:stages-network} details these changes and their corresponding
temporal stages from 2010 to 2019. The stages were grouped based on the
time interval between changes, with modifications occurring within six
months being grouped together in the same stage. This criteria follows
previous empirical studies and assumes that accessibility benefits are
perceived as simultaneous when changes happen within short timeframes
(Ahlfeldt 2013; Gibbons and Machin 2005).

\subsubsection{Spatial analytical
framework}\label{spatial-analytical-framework}

Various spatial analytical schemes (SAS) are considered to address the
research questions related to the MAUP. The area of study is
systematically partitioned in uniform cells, \(j\), of various gradual
sizes to examine the scale effects, drawing on Wong, Lasus, and Falk
(1999). The criteria and implementation employed is adopted from
empirical accessibility studies, using hexagonal cells of 0.5 km, 1 km,
2 km, and 4 km (Pereira 2018; Pereira et al. 2019). This is to (i)
maintain consistency in this research area, (ii) allow comparability,
(iii) exploit existing analytical tools and resources and, (iv) augment
previous findings in a different context. A further scheme defined by
the official post code zones was considered to account for zoning.

Table \ref{tab:sas-summary} summarises the main characteristics of each

km\textsuperscript{2} and 13.5 km\textsuperscript{2}. On average, the

\subsection{Data}\label{data}

\subsubsection{Demographic}\label{demographic}

The demographic comes from the 2010 Population Census (INEGI 2012).
These data were selected for their detailed disaggregation at the city
block level, including key fields such as total population and average
years of education completed by individuals aged 15 and older.

The count data included were transferred to the SAS using the
\emph{areal weighting method} (Saporito et al. 2007). This reassigns the
data according to the proportional overlay of the source and target
areas. The average education level was assigned using the
\emph{population weighting method}. In addition, this takes into
consideration the number of inhabitants and their type.

\subsubsection{Employment}\label{employment}

Employment data are sourced from the 2014 Economic Census (\emph{Censos
Económicos 2014}) (INEGI 2015b) and accessed at the city block level via
the Microdata Lab and Remote Processing service
(\url{https://www.inegi.org.mx/microdatos/}). The dataset includes: 1.
Number of establishments (fixed locations producing goods or services);
2. Employed personnel (individuals active in establishments, including
volunteers and family members), referred to as `employment'; and 3.
Total gross remuneration paid for the relevant year.

Employment information, which came aggregated at a higher geographic
level than the city-block due to privacy concerns (\textless8\%), were
re-allocated using proportional estimates based on the spatial
distribution from the 2015 DENUE (INEGI 2015a). This is an open-access,
point-level dataset of individual establishments. The employment data
was also assigned from city blocks to the SAS following the areal
weighting method (Saporito et al. 2007).

The average income was computed for each areal unit based on the total
number of employment positions and the total gross remuneration paid.

\subsubsection{Public transport
timetables}\label{public-transport-timetables}

The estimated travel time used in the accessibility measures is based on
scheduled public transport services. The Transport Authority in Mexico
City (\emph{Secretaría de Movilidad de la Ciudad de México}, Semovi)
released the first \emph{general transit feed specification} (GTFS)
files in 2013, which were accessed via the Open Mobility website
(\url{https://openmobilitydata.org/}). These datasets cover the agencies
operating most MPTN services in GMC. However, there are some
limitations: firstly, the geographic coverage primarily includes
services within Ciudad de México, excluding BRT services provided by
MXB. Secondly, the temporal coverage starts in 2013, with updates
occurring only sporadically, approximately once a year.

To overcome the first limitation, the geographic coverage of the
official GTFS data was augmented to include three BRT corridors operated
by MXB. This was carried out \emph{manually} based on unstructured
information provided by the Transport Agency of the State of Mexico
(\emph{Sistema de Transporte Masivo y Teleferico del Estado de México},
Sitramytem). This corresponded to services for 2019 only.

Regarding the second limitation, the MPTN's temporal stages were
reconstructed using the GTFS standard. The primary input for each stage
was the closest available official GTFS data reflecting changes in the
MPTN, with only the relevant services for each corridor in their
respective stages being retained. For MXB services, 2019 data was
considered due to the lack of earlier details, assuming uniform
characteristics of the BRT corridors across the stages. Similarly, for
other corridors included in early stages, services were assumed to
reflect 2013 levels due to the unavailability of earlier data. All
non-MPTN services were excluded, thereby focusing exclusively on the
benefits of the MPTN.

\subsubsection{Road network and pedestrian
infrastructure}\label{road-network-and-pedestrian-infrastructure}

The road and pedestrian infrastructure network is sourced from
\emph{OpenStreetMap} (OSM) (Manually downloaded on 30th October 2019
through a web export tool available in
\url{https://export.hotosm.org/es/v3/}{]}). This enables multi-modal
journeys to realistically represent the access/egress stage to a public
transport service when combining walking and public transport.

\subsection{MPTN travel times and impedance
parameters}\label{mptn-travel-times-and-impedance-parameters}

\subsubsection{Modeled travel time}\label{modeled-travel-time}

Travel time matrices were calculated for each origin-destination (OD)
pair across each SAS using a combination of walking and public transport
within the main public transport network. OD points \(j\) and \(k\),
respectively, are represented by population-weighted centroids of
spatial units containing economic activity (from the 2014 Economic
Census) or demographic data (from the 2010 Population Census). Travel
times are capped at 180 minutes, based on patterns observed in the 2017
Travel Survey.

Travel times were modelled using a door-to-door approach with the
\texttt{r5r} software package for the \texttt{R} programming language
(Saraiva et al. 2021; R Core Team 2021). This approach considers access,
waiting, onboard, transfer, and egress times. All-to-all travel time
matrices were generated for each SAS across seven temporal stages using
reconstructed GTFS files and road/pedestrian networks. The estimated
travel time considers the median time within a three-hour time window
between 8 a.m. and 11 a.m. The maximum walking distance to access and
egress public transport or in walking journeys only is limited to 40
minutes, approximately 3.2 km at an average walking speed of 4.8 km/h.

The internal-zone travel-times were estimated based on the surface area
using: \(d_{j=k} = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{ \frac{area_j}{\pi}}\) (Frost and
Spence 1995; Stepniak and Rosik 2015). The metric distance was
transformed to travel time using the average speed modelled for short
journeys (less than 3 km).

\subsubsection{Spatial decay function and calibration of accessibility
parameters}\label{spatial-decay-function-and-calibration-of-accessibility-parameters}

The spatial decay function and parameters for the accessibility measures
were determined using estimates from a trip distribution model,
following Handy and Niemeier (1997). This analysis used data from the
2017 Travel Survey (INEGI 2018), focusing on a weekday and journeys
including public transport or walking.

Alternative specifications were estimated within a doubly-constrained
gravity model, expressed as:

\begin{equation}
  T_{jk} = A_j O_j B_k D_k f(c_{jk})
  \label{eq:gravity-model}
\end{equation}

where \begin{equation} 
  A_j = \left[ \sum{B_k D_k f(c_{jk})} \right]^{-1}
\end{equation}

and \begin{equation} 
  B_k = \left[ \sum{A_j O_j f(c_{jk}) }  \right] ^{-1}
\end{equation}

with balancing factors \(A_j\) and \(B_k\) calculated iteratively.
\(T_{jk}\) are commuter flows between origins (\(j\)) and destinations
(\(k\)), with constraints on the number of journeys at each, i.e.,
\(O_j\) and \(D_k\). \(f(c_{jk})\) is a spatial deterrence or decay
function. The inverse power (\(c_{jk}^{-\beta}\)) and negative
exponential (\(e^{(-\beta c_{jk})}\)) decay functions were tested.
\(\beta\) represents the deterrence parameter. \(c_{jk}\) is the
observed travel time.

A modified specification aimed at capturing an start-up cost for
inter-zone journeys or analogously a benefit of residing and working
within the same zone is also considered (Thorsen and Gitlesen 1998).
This includes a parameter \(\mu\) which affects only the diagonal
elements of the OD matrix. The term is given by the following
expression:

\begin{equation} 
  \mu \delta_{jk}
\end{equation}

where Kronecker delta takes the following value:

\begin{equation}
  \delta_{ij} \left\{
    \begin{array}{ll}
        0 & \quad \text{if   } j \neq k \\
        1 & \quad \text{if   } j = k \\
    \end{array} 
   \right.\\
\end{equation}

The simultaneous calibration of the model parameters \(\beta\) and
\(\mu\) is achieved via optimization by minimising the standardised root
mean square error (SRMSE) using the \emph{Nelder-Mead's simplex
algorithm} as implemented in base \texttt{R} (R Core Team 2021).

The preliminary analyses shows that the negative exponential function
provided better fitting estimates compared to the inverse power. Also,
the use of the within-zone parameter \(\mu\) improves the model fit.

Therefore, the selected decay function used in the accessibility
measures in Equation \ref{eq:empirical-access-general} and Equation
\ref{eq:empirical-access-matching} is the modified negative exponential
including \(\mu\), as shown below:

\begin{equation} 
  f(c_{jkt}) = e^{(-\beta c^{PT}_{jkt} + \mu \delta_{jk})}
\label{eq:seleected-decaying-function}
\end{equation}

where, \(c^{PT}_{jkt}\) is the modelled travel time from \(j\) to \(k\)
at the MPTN temporal stage \(t\). The optimized parameters used are
\(\beta = 0.031\) and \(\mu = 1.662\).

\subsection{Analytical framework}\label{analytical-framework}

\subsubsection{Accessibility indicators}\label{accessibility-indicators}

To focus on the key spatial differences between generic and matching
accessibility measures and the role of the MAUP, the first section of
the analysis summarises the level accessibility over the period of study
as following:

\begin{equation} 
  A^{All}_{j} = \text{median}(A^{All}_{jt}) \text{ for } t= 1,2,..,7,
\label{eq:median-accessibility-all}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation} 
  A^{Matching}_{jm} = \text{median}(A^{Matching}_{jtm}) \text{ for } t= 1,2,..,7. 
\label{eq:median-accessibility-matching}
\end{equation}

A similarity rate is computed to compare the All and Matching measures
as following:

\begin{equation} 
  \text{Similarity rate} = \frac{  A^{Matching}_{jm} } { A^{All}_{j} }.
\label{eq:similarity-rate}
\end{equation}

This ranges between 0 and 1, where values near to 0 indicate low
similarity and 1 a high similarity rate.

\subsubsection{Accessibility equity
measures}\label{accessibility-equity-measures}

For the transport equity evaluation of infrastructure projects, the
prevalence of accessibility shortfalls measure is adopted from Martens
and Bastiaanssen (2019). This is expressed by the equation below:

\begin{equation}
  H_t = \sum^{P}_{j=1} I(A_{it} < z)
\label{eq:accessibility-shortfalls}
\end{equation}

Where, \(H_t\) represents the number of people whose accessibility level
falls below the established accessibility sufficiency threshold at
temporal stage \(t\), \(P\) is the total population, \(A_{jt}\) is the
accessibility level of at location \(j\) and temporal stage \(t\), \(z\)
is the sufficiency threshold, and \(I(A_{it} < z)\) is an indicator
function that equals 1 if \(y_{it}\) is less than the sufficiency
threshold (\(z\)), and 0 otherwise. We also use the headcount ratio
\(P_0 = H_t / N\), where a larger share indicates a less fair transport
system. Given that this is a standardised measure, we can compare the
different temporal stages.

We use a range of sufficiency thresholds \(z\) using the 10, 15, 20, and
30 percentile of accessibility by car for each corresponding measure,
i.e., All or Matching. Accessibility by car is estimated using Equation
\ref{eq:empirical-access-general} and Equation
\ref{eq:empirical-access-matching} where
\(f(c_{jk}) = \text{exp}(-\beta c^{Car}_{jk})\). \(c^{Car}_{jk}\) is the
estimated travel time by car. This approach draws on Martens and
Bastiaanssen (2019).

\subsubsection{Indicators of spatial
association}\label{indicators-of-spatial-association}

Two spatial statistics techniques are used to measure the spatial
difference between the All and Matching measures. The first, the Global
Moran's I statistic, which measures spatial autocorrelation, is
expressed as (Moraga 2023):

\[I = \frac{N \sum_{j} \sum_{k} w_{jk} (x_j - \bar{x}) (x_k - \bar{x})}{\left( \sum_{j \neq k} w_{jk} \right) \sum_{j} (x_j - \bar{x})^2}
\] Where, \(N\) is the number of individual units in each SAS, \(x_j\)
and \(x_k\) are the accessibility levels at location \(j\) and \(k\),
respectively, \(\bar{x}\) is the mean accessibility, \(w_{jk}\)
represents a binary spatial weight between locations \(i\) and \(j\),
and the term \(\sum^N_{j=1} \sum^N_{k=1}\) is the sum of the spatial
weights. \(w_{jk}\) is an element of the spatial weight matrix \(W\),
which was computed based on 6-nearest neighbours of the OD points with
the \texttt{spdep} package (Bivand 2021).

Local Moran's I is used to account for local indicators of spatial
association (LISA), defined as:

\[I_j = \frac{N (x_j - \bar{x})}{\sum_{k} (x_k - \bar{x})^2} \sum_{k} w_{jk} (x_k - \bar{x})
\]

A high value for \(I_j\) suggests that the area is surrounded by areas
with similar values, indicating that it is part of a cluster of high
observations, low observations, or moderate observations. Conversely, a
low value implies that the area is surrounded by dissimilar values,
marking it as an outlier and indicating that the observation of area
\(j\) differs from most or all of its neighbours.

We compute a map of p-values that represent the probability of exceeding
the observed values under the null hypothesis, allowing for the
identification of significant clusters.

\ref{fig:relative-change} shows moderate increases in Stage 4
(2013-2015) and Stage 5 (2015-2016), while the matching measure records
little to no change, and even a slight decline during these stages. This
divergence suggests that while the infrastructure improvements
introduced in these periods allowed access to additional employment from
certain residential zones, the jobs at destinations did not match the
educational qualifications in the areas of influence.

We also draw on the results from a transport equity perspective. An
advantage is that it can distinguish between populations that were
already served at an `adequate' level before an intervention and those
who were not. Figure \ref{fig:headcount-lineplot} shows the head count
ratio which are under various minimal accessibility thresholds over the
MPTN temporal stages for the `All' and `Matching' measures. From the
plot, a general reduction in the population under the accessibility
thresholds can be observed. This is in the direction expected.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../output/plots_maps/exploratory/equity_overtime.png}
  \caption{Equity analysis over various temporal stages. S = Stage.}
  \label{fig:headcount-lineplot}
\end{figure}

There are additional insights to draw from the results. First, the scale
of the ratio is smaller for the matching measure, with the gap narrowing
as the percentile increases. This relates to the different density
distributions of the measures, where the `Matching' measure is more
skewed than the `All' measure. Still, both measures show parallel trends
for most stages, with a few exceptions. For example, the `All' measure
consistently declines between Stage 1 (`Before 2010') and Stage 4 (`2013
to 2015') across all thresholds, while the `Matching' measure only
begins to reflect this trend after the 15th percentile, showing similar
trends by the 30th percentile.

Another distinction is that the `Matching' measure shows significant
improvement in Stage 5 (`2015 to 2016') for all thresholds, which is not
captured by the `All' measure. This improvement corresponds to the BRT
corridor MXB-LII, located in the periphery of the MPTN and connecting
key corridors in the northern areas of the city. This suggests that some
northern populations were previously considered well-served by the
generic measure, even though the employment opportunities did not match
their qualifications. This means that the opening of MXB-LII enabled
access to matching employment opportunities for those who already had
access to a minimum number of general opportunities.

The global population-weighted average in Figure
\ref{fig:relative-change} highlights large accessibility benefits
introduced in Stage 7 (2018-2019). However, the equity analysis results
in Figure \ref{fig:headcount-lineplot} show consistent negligible
impacts across all percentiles and measures in this stage. This is
because the MB-L7, implemented during this stage, is centrally located
within the MPTN and primarily serves to articulate existing network
corridors. As a result, the additional accessibility to employment was
concentrated in areas that already exceeded the examined thresholds.

Figure \ref{fig:equity-barplot} shows the net population taken out from
various accessibility thresholds by education quintile across the MPTN
stages. Between Stage 2 and Stage 4, the groups benefiting from the
infrastructure are concentrated in the intermediate quantiles, a trend
more pronounced in the general accessibility measure. The matching
measure remains consistent for some stages, particularly for the 30th
percentile threshold. Interestingly, the matching measure reveals a
progressive distributional effect in Stage 5, where the benefits go to
lower-educated groups, between the first and third quintile. This makes
sense, since the MXB-LIII enabled direct connection to the MPTN to two
densely populated municipalities characterised by high-deprivation
levels, i.e.~\emph{Ciudad Nezahualcóyotl} and \emph{Chimalhuacan}.
However, these effects diminish at higher accessibility thresholds,
likely because low-educated populations reside in less central areas.
Thus, as the threshold increases, lower-educated zones fall short, while
areas with higher education levels are considered beneficiaries.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../output/plots_maps/exploratory/equity_barplot.png}
  \caption{Equity analysis by education level over various temporal stages.}
  \label{fig:equity-barplot}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:equity-barplot} also highlights the matching employment
benefits in Stage 5 (2015-2016), as discussed earlier. Interestingly,
these benefits primarily went to the more educated population between
the third and fifth quintiles, a pattern consistent across all
thresholds. As noted, no significant population was taken out of the
minimal accessibility thresholds in Stages 6 and 7.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Discussion
\label{sec:discussion}}{Discussion }}\label{discussion}

The results examining the spatial differences between generic and
matching accessibility measures showed important interactions with the
MAUP. Specifically, the larger the scale, the less relevant the
distinction of matching accessibility becomes, as indicated by the
increased correlation. To some extent, this is explained by the tendency
of larger units to emphasise within-zone opportunities, given the
increased travel costs for residents to leave the unit or reach another
one. Thus, at larger scales, accessibility measures tend to reflect
employment density. Interestingly, the postcode retained matching
employment information to a very good extent, as evidenced by the low
correlation. By implication, and as shown visually, the role of public
transport infrastructure gradually diminishes with the use of larger
scales. Similarly, postcode units demonstrated a retention of some of
these features. Analysts who aim to account for matching characteristics
and the role of transport infrastructure should take these effects into
consideration.

Furthermore, our results indicate that zones with significant similarity
between the `All' and `Matching' measures were predominantly composed of
the most educated population. This reflects a East-West divide, aligning
with the well-documented spatial inequalities in GMC, such as those
related to household income and car ownership (Guerra 2015), healthy
food environments (Ortega-Avila 2023), and social mix (Letouzé et al.
2022). From an urban economic perspective, highly educated populations
are likely to be in a position to afford residential locations that
maximise accessibility to employment opportunities they consider
valuable, outbidding less affluent groups (Duranton and Puga 2015). An
implication for accessibility analyses that draw on the assumption of
equivalent attractiveness of employment is that they might
systematically overestimate the relevant opportunities available to less
educated populations. This is expected to be particularly relevant in
contexts with higher economic inequalities, following urban land-rent
theory.

The results regarding the evaluation of public transport infrastructure
over time are varied. Firstly, it is confirmed that a simple measure
alone, such as the global average of accessibility, might overlook key
transport equity insights (Karner, Pereira, and Farber 2024).
Specifically, while certain extensions of the MPTN reflect a substantial
improvement on average, an equity analysis from a sufficiency
perspective shows negligible effects. We argue that both indicators
supplement each other. For instance, the results highlight a
concentration of the benefits in Stage 7 among populations that were
already well served. This finding was consistent regardless of the
accessibility measure being used.

Secondly, both accessibility measures point in similar directions with
parallel trends at a higher level. However, there are particularities to
note. The matching accessibility measure was more sensitive to
sufficiency thresholds in the equity analysis. This may be related to
the observation that the distribution of the baseline used to define the
thresholds presents a more skewed distribution.

The comparison between measures also showed some complementarity. For
example, the fact that the matching measure reflects that there are
populations benefited by particular infrastructure interventions, while
the general measure does not, implies that certain group might have
received `general' accessibility improvements previously and, later,
specific ones related to matching employment opportunities. Both
indicators are useful when considering the former measure as a form of
\emph{generalized access to urban opportunities}, as the location of
employment often reflects the provision of a service (Merlin 2017;
Levinson and Wu 2020). Meanwhile, a distinction of matching employment
opportunities is useful when applied to commuting to work patterns or
labour market inequalities, for example.

Thirdly, the particular differences arising from the inclusion of
matching opportunities in equity analyses vary with a range of outcomes
from case to case. It is shown that the implementation of a transport
project can potentially imply benefits either for well-educated or
low-educated groups only, or that measures can be consistent at times.
In the area of study, this is observed to be related to the location of
the transport improvement with respect to the overall transport network
and the demographic characteristics of the population in proximity to
the public transport services. This reinforces the important role of
accessibility measures in public transport evaluation, given that they
are able to capture key elements of the transport system and
characteristics of the population simultaneously. Thus, it is
recommended to use both measures to form comprehensive insights into the
impact of transport infrastructure on a case-based basis.

\subsection{Limitations and future
work}\label{limitations-and-future-work}

The present study comes with the following limitations. Specifically,
the analyses assume that all populations within zones share similar
demographic characteristics, e.g., the same education levels or
employment opportunities. This is a broader issue in geography discussed
under the `Ecological Fallacy' literature (Walker 2021). This is also
related to limitations of data, given that individual-level data for
both jobs and residents are restricted to maintain privacy. However,
this is expected to have minor implications because the original source
information is aggregated in small geographic units, the urban block.
Additionally, the modelled matching access measure used assumes limited
social mobility, allowing only equal education-income quintiles to be
matched. Additional research in different contexts can clarify the
sensitivity of this assumption. Furthermore, the analyses focus on the
MPTN only, which overlooks street transit services. In the case of the
study, this is largely constrained by the information available, given
that comprehensive structured timetable data is not accessible.

Future work is recommended to examine the sensitivity of results when
using individual-level job characteristics to establish a match between
residents and opportunities. Similar analyses in a diversity of contexts
with a range of social and economic disparities would help to identify
consistent patterns. This could help to establish the extent of the
findings in our empirical case. Furthermore, additional work should
examine the assumptions of matching measures and their relevance for
specific applications, e.g.~in the housing market or commuting to work
patterns.

\FloatBarrier

\section{\texorpdfstring{Conclusions
\label{sec:conclusions}}{Conclusions }}\label{conclusions}

The present study relaxed a common assumption of accessibility analysis
that access to employment is equally attractive to all types of
residents. The study examines the specific differences between a generic
and matching accessibility measures taking into the account the MAUP,
and its relevance for equity assessments of public transport
infrastructure.

It is shown that both components of the modifiable areal unit problem,
namely scale and zoning, play an important role in this distinction.
Specifically, the added information regarding the matching employment is
gradually smoothed with increased scale. Also, the difference between
measures is sensitive to zoning as postcodes, for example, can
over-represent the role or large zones in local spatial analyses.

A sensitivity analysis is recommended particularly in context with high
social inequality. This is because the allocation of residential land
use might push low-income residents more acutely with the consequence of
the level of access to relevant employment being systematically
underestimated for less advantaged populations. Also, the interpretation
and information provided by the distinct measures will depend on
specific applications. In this study, we have shown that both measures
play complementary roles.
