\begin{abstract}
The definition of accessibility emphasises the role of the
attractiveness of opportunities at potential destinations that residents
consider valuable. This study revises the common assumption in empirical
studies that residents are equally attracted to all types of employment
and examines its implications for public transport evaluation.
Additionally, the role of the modifiable areal unit problem is also
explored in this relationship.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{
    Accessibility
   \and
    Employment matching
   \and
    Public transport infrastructure
   \and
    Modifiable areal unit problem
  }

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Location-based accessibility measures are valuable for assessing the
benefits enabled by transport infrastructure, among others, those in the
property market and social equity. At its core, accessibility concerns
the ease of travel to opportunities that people consider valuable. This
study revises the implications of the common assumption in empirical
studies that residents are equally attracted to all types of employment.
We focus on employment because it is one of the most frequent purpose
for travelling. Additionally, the role of the modifiable areal unit
problem (MAUP) is examined in this context. These considerations can
provide valuable guidance for measuring accessibility in environmental
valuation studies or for equity assessments of public transport
infrastructure, for example. Thus, the following questions are examined:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  What are the key spatial differences between generic and matching
  accessibility measures and what is the role of the MAUP?
\item
  How does the evaluation of PT infrastructure differs when accounting
  for matching employment opportunities in accessibility measures?
\item
  What are the social equity implications of the above?
\end{enumerate}

This paper contributes to literature by:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Examining the implications of relaxing the assumption that all
  employment opportunities are equally attractive to all the population
  in accessibility measures drawing on an empirical case for a ten-year
  period.
\item
  Examining the role of the MAUP in these effects.
\item
  Reports the findings of GMC, an understudied mega-city, by identifying
  and digitally reconstructing in a common format (general transit feed
  specification, GTFS) a series of temporal stages of the MPTN in GMC
  for a ten-year period (2009-2019).
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature review}\label{literature-review}

Thériault, Voisin, and Des Rosiers (2013) conceptualize accessibility as
``the ability that persons living in that area can, within an acceptable
travel time, reach a satisfactory diversity of activity locations that
are important to them'' (p.~234). Levinson and Wu (2020) refer to
accessibility measures simply as ``the ease of reaching valued
destinations'' (p.~130).

\subsection{Accessibility measures}\label{accessibility-measures}

Location-based measures consist of the sum of the product of two
functions, namely \(g(\cdot)\) and \(f(\cdot)\), as shown in Equation
\ref{eq:location-based1} (Páez, Scott, and Morency 2012):

\begin{equation}
   A^p_{ik} =  \sum_j g(W_{jk}) f(c^p_{ij}).
  \label{eq:location-based1}
\end{equation}

Here, accessibility \(A\) to opportunities of type \(k\) is viewed from
location \(i\), from the perspective of person type \(p\). The measure
reflects the attractiveness of opportunities \(W\) of type \(k\) at
destination \(j\). The term \(c_{ij}\) refers to a function of travel
cost between \(i\) and \(j\), as experienced by person \(p\).

Equation \ref{eq:location-based1} is often used in literature to measure
accessibility to employment as following:

\begin{equation}
  A_{i} = \sum^N_{i=1} E_j f(c_{ij})
  \label{eq:access-employment-general}
\end{equation}

The attractiveness of a destination \(W\) is expressed by \(E\), usually
as the number of employment opportunities at \(j\), i.e.~\(E_j\). In
\(f(c_{ij})\), travel cost is represented with some form of travel time.

In Equation \ref{eq:access-employment-general}, households and
opportunities are assumed to be homogeneous, i.e., all types of
employment are equally attractive for all types of residents. However,
contributions from theory highlight the characteristics of the demand
(residents at origins) and supply at potential destinations (Geurs and
van Wee 2004; Levinson and Wu 2020). Specifically, Levinson and Wu state
that ``different job categories represent different economic activities,
and different types of jobs generally require different skills and
education levels'' (2020, 147). In the context of the labour market,
this is supported by the Human Capital Theory (Schultz 1961; Becker
1994).

Location-based measures can consider these elements. For instance,
Equation \ref{eq:location-based1} refers to the following components:
(1) individual's profiles; 2) how different profiles perceive or
experience the space, and; 3) constraints that may prevent segments of
the population to access some type of activities. Some of these aspects
have been found to be relevant in empirical studies. For instance,
gender in Osland (2010) or age and gender in Thériault, Voisin, and Des
Rosiers (2013) in the study of the property prices.

The operationalization of these measures can vary. Levinson and Wu
(2020) suggest to weight employment in each category with the number of
eligible workers. In empirical studies, these characteristics can be
expressed as type of person \(m\) at \(j\) and type of employment \(m\)
at \(k\) (Pereira, Grégoire, and Karner 2019). Accordingly, the measure
can be specified as following:

\begin{equation}
  A_{im} = \sum^N_{i=1} E_{im} f(c_{ij}),
   \label{eq:access-matching}
\end{equation}

Here, only employment opportunities \(E\) matching the profile of
residents between \(j\) and \(k\) are considered in the accessibility
measure. Thus, this reflects the notion that residents would find
attractive only employment opportunities that match their skills and
education level with corresponding employment opportunities.

\subsubsection{Evaluation of public
transport}\label{evaluation-of-public-transport}

Location-based accessibility measures have been used to evaluate the
performance of public transport systems in policy report and research
(Ermagun 2021; Liu, Porr, and Miller 2022; Conway, Byrd, and Van
Eggermond 2018; Malekzadeh and Chung 2020; Palmateer et al. 2021). A
measure based on realistic travel times can reflect important elements
of a public transport system. This is because travel time by public
transport is affected by several aspects of the system, such as:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The topology of the network, e.g.~connectivity, centrality.
\item
  Capacity of the infrastructure, e.g.~degree of right of way
  segregation, capacity and design of stations.
\item
  Spatial coverage, e.g.~availability of infrastructure and catchment
  areas.
\item
  Operational characteristics, e.g.~frequency of services, overlapping
  routes, express services, transfer wait times.
\end{itemize}

Despite the growing relevance of accessibility measures in the
evaluation of public transport infrastructure, knowledge of the
implications of overlooking matching employment are limited in
literature.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Accessibility measures
examined}\label{accessibility-measures-examined}

The general accessibility is computed as following for this work:

\begin{equation}
  A^{\text{PT}}_{jt} = \sum^J_{j=1} E_k f(d_{jkt})
   \label{eq:empirical-access-general}
\end{equation}

The implementation of matching accessibility is adapted according to the
information available for the area of study and some preliminary
findings. The households' education level (i.e., median number of years
of education) is employed assuming correspondence with the level of
salary paid in the employment market. This is further supported by the
positive relationship found between education level and wages in
empirical studies in the context of Mexico (Nuñez, Paredes, and
Garduño-Rivera 2017; Rojas, Angulo, and Velázquez 2000).

\begin{equation}
  A^{\text{PT}}_{jtm} = \sum^J_{j=1} E_{km} f(d_{jkt}),
   \label{eq:empirical-access-matching}
\end{equation}

Here, \(m\) at \(j\) is categorized according to the median education
level of its residents by quintile and \(m\) at \(k\) is classified
based on the average paid salary by quintile. Only employment
opportunities \(E\) matching the education level quantile between \(j\)
and \(k\) are considered (when \(j \ne k\)).\footnote{An exception
  occurs when \(j = k\). In this case, employment opportunities in the
  origin are considered as available. This is to avoid the assumption
  implying that some zones have accessibility values equal to 0} This
assumes that residents would deem valuable only employment opportunities
that match their education level, i.e.~those in the same quintile. The
data sources and specific procedures to process the data are described
in the next sub-section.

\subsection{Area of study}\label{area-of-study}

Greater Mexico City (GMC) (\emph{Zona Metropolitana del Valle de
México}) is located in in the south-centre of Mexico. In 2015, GMC had
nearly 21 million inhabitants (INEGI 2015b). The official delimitation
consists of 76 adjacent municipalities, which are part of three
different states, namely: (1) \emph{Ciudad de México} (16
municipalities); (2) \emph{Hidalgo} (1 municipality), and (3)
\emph{México} (59 municipalities), as shown in Figure
\ref{fig:metro-map}. The total GMC area is 7,889 km\textsuperscript{2},
and it is home to 17\% of the national population.

\subsubsection{The main public transport network
(MPTN)}\label{the-main-public-transport-network-mptn}

The focus of this work is on \emph{rapid transit} and \emph{semi-rapid
transit} infrastructure, excluding specialized modes i.e.~\emph{aerial
tramway}. The combination of public transport modes included in the
analyses is referred to as the \emph{main public transport network}
(MPTN). Figure \ref{fig:metro-map} shows the MPTN in GMC for the year
2019. This consists of a variety of modes operated by the following main
agencies: The metro system, directly operated by the state-owned company
\emph{Sistema de Transporte Colectivo} (STC) and designated as Metro;
two BRT systems, namely \emph{Metrobus} (MB) in Ciudad de México and
Mexibus (MXB) in the state of México; One LRT line operated by STE; and
one regional rail line designated as \emph{Sub}.

The period of study was divided into seven temporal stages to reflect
significant changes in the MPTN over time. Initially, changes were
identified by the physical alterations in the MPTN, such as the opening
of a new corridor or the extension of an existing one. Table
\ref{tab:stages-network} details these changes and their corresponding
temporal stages from 2010 to 2019. The stages were grouped based on the
time interval between changes, with modifications occurring within six
months being grouped together in the same stage. This criteria follows
previous empirical studies and assumes that accessibility benefits are
perceived as simultaneous when changes happen within short timeframes
(Ahlfeldt 2013; Gibbons and Machin 2005).

\subsubsection{Spatial analytical
framework}\label{spatial-analytical-framework}

Various spatial analytical schemes (SAS) are considered to address the
research questions related to the MAUP. The area of study is
systematically partitioned in uniform cells, \(j\), of various gradual
sizes to examine the scale effects, drawing on Wong, Lasus, and Falk
(1999). The criteria and implementation employed is adopted from
empirical accessibility studies, using hexagonal cells of 0.5 km, 1 km,
2 km, and 4 km (Pereira 2018, 2019). This is to (1) maintain consistency
in this research area, (2) allow comparability, (3) exploit existing
analytical tools and resources and, (4) augment previous findings in a
different context. A further scheme defined by the official post code
zones was considered to account for zoning.

Table \ref{tab:sas-summary} summarises the main characteristics of each
SAS for cells containing residents or economic activity. The total
number of cells ranges from 13,300 in the highest resolution grid (0.5
km) to 410 in the lowest (4 km), with surface areas varying between 0.22
km\textsuperscript{2} and 13.5 km\textsuperscript{2}. On average, the
smallest cells contain only 1,500 inhabitants, while the largest have
over 50,000.

\subsection{Data}\label{data}

\subsubsection{Demographic}\label{demographic}

The demographic comes from the 2010 Population Census (INEGI 2012).
These data were selected for their detailed disaggregation at the city
block level, including key fields such as total population and average
years of education completed by individuals aged 15 and older.

The count data included were transferred to the SAS using the
\emph{areal weighting method} (Saporito et al. 2007). This reassigns the
data according to the proportional overlay of the source and target
areas. The average education level was assigned using the
\emph{population weighting method}. In addition, this takes into
consideration the number of inhabitants and their type.

\subsubsection{Employment}\label{employment}

Employment data are sourced from the 2014 Economic Census (\emph{Censos
Económicos 2014}) (INEGI 2015b) and accessed at the city block level via
the Microdata Lab and Remote Processing service
(\url{https://www.inegi.org.mx/microdatos/}). The dataset includes: 1.
Number of establishments (fixed locations producing goods or services);
2. Employed personnel (individuals active in establishments, including
volunteers and family members), referred to as `employment'; and 3.
Total gross remuneration paid for the relevant year.

Employment information which came aggregated at higher geographic level
than the city-block due to privacy (\textless8\%) were re-allocated
using proportional estimates based on the spatial distribution from the
2015 DENUE (INEGI 2015a). This is an open-access, point-level dataset of
individual establishments. The employment data was assigned from city
blocks to SASs following the same approach employed for the demographic
data, i.e.~according to the areal weighting method.

The average income was computed for each areal unit based on the total
number of employment and the total gross remuneration paid.

\subsubsection{Public transport
timetables}\label{public-transport-timetables}

The estimated travel time used in accessibility measures is based on
scheduled public transport services. The Transport Authority in Mexico
City (\emph{Secretaría de Movilidad de la Ciudad de México}, Semovi)
released the first \emph{general transit feed specification} (GTFS)
files in 2013, which were accessed via the Open Mobility website
(\url{https://openmobilitydata.org/}). These datasets cover the agencies
operating most MPTN services in GMC. However, there are some
limitations: firstly, the geographic coverage primarily includes
services within Ciudad de México, excluding BRT services provided by
MXB. Secondly, the temporal coverage starts in 2013, with updates
occurring only sporadically, approximately once a year.

To overcome the first limitation, the geographic coverage of the
official GTFS data was augmented to include three BRT corridors operated
by MXB. This was carried out \emph{manually} based on unstructured
information provided by the Transport Agency of the State of Mexico
(\emph{Sistema de Transporte Masivo y Teleferico del Estado de México},
Sitramytem). This corresponded to services for 2019 only.

Regarding the second limitation, the MPTN's temporal stages were
reconstructed using the GTFS standard. The primary input for each stage
was the closest available official GTFS data reflecting changes in the
MPTN, with only the relevant services for each corridor in their
respective stages being retained. For MXB services, 2019 data was
considered due to the lack of earlier details, assuming uniform
characteristics of the BRT corridors across the stages. Similarly, for
other corridors included in early stages, services were assumed to
reflect 2013 levels due to the unavailability of earlier data. All
non-MPTN services were excluded, thereby focusing exclusively on the
benefits of the MPTN.

\subsubsection{Road network and pedestrian
infrastructure}\label{road-network-and-pedestrian-infrastructure}

The road and pedestrian infrastructure network is sourced from
\emph{OpenStreetMap} (OSM) (Manually downloaded on 30th October 2019
through a web export tool available in
\url{https://export.hotosm.org/es/v3/}{]}). This enables multi-modal
journeys to realistically represent the access/egress stage to a public
transport service when combining walking and public transport.

\subsection{Travel costs and impedance
parameters}\label{travel-costs-and-impedance-parameters}

\subsubsection{Modeled travel time}\label{modeled-travel-time}

Travel time matrices were calculated for each origin-destination (OD)
pair across SASs using a combination of walking and public transport
within the main public transport network. OD points \(i\) and \(j\) are
represented by population-weighted centroids of spatial units containing
economic activity (from the 2014 Economic Census) or demographic data
(from the 2010 Population Census). Travel times are capped at 180
minutes, based on patterns observed in the 2017 Travel Survey.

Travel times were modelled using a door-to-door approach with
OpenTripPlanner (OTP) version `1.4.0' (OTP n.d.) via the implementation
of an open-access routine written \texttt{Python} (Pereira et al. 2019).
This approach considers access, waiting, onboard, transfer, and egress
times. All-to-all travel time matrices were generated for each SAS
across seven temporal stages using reconstructed GTFS files and
road/pedestrian networks, with eight random departure times between 10
a.m. and 2 p.m. The median travel time was used for accessibility models
(Conway, Byrd, and van der Linden 2017). This process, computationally
intensive due to the high resolution of SASs, resulted in 2.1 billion
routes. Matrices were then merged, and median travel times calculated,
with walking distances limited to 3 km when using public transport and
4.8 km (60 minutes) for walking-only trips.

The internal-zone travel-times were estimated based on the surface area
as following: \(d_{i=j} = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{ \frac{area_i}{\pi}}\)
(Frost and Spence 1995; Stepniak and Rosik 2015). The metric distance
was transformed to travel time using the average speed modelled for
short journeys (less than 3 km).

\subsubsection{Spatial decaying function and calibration of
accessibility
parameters}\label{spatial-decaying-function-and-calibration-of-accessibility-parameters}

The spatial decaying function and parameters for accessibility measures
were determined using estimates from a trip distribution model,
following Handy and Niemeier (1997). This model is used to understand
how commuters in Greater Mexico City (GMC) discount employment
opportunities based on travel time. The analyses use data from the 2017
Travel Survey, focusing on a weekday and public transport combined with
walking.

Alternative specifications were estimated within a doubly constrained
gravity model framework, expressed as following:

\begin{equation}
  T_{jk} = A_j O_j B_k D_k f(d_{jk})
  \label{eq:gravity-model}
\end{equation}

where \begin{equation} 
  A_j = \left[ \sum{B_i D_i f(d_{jk})} \right]^{-1}
\end{equation}

and \begin{equation} 
  B_k = \left[ \sum{A_k O_k f(d_{jk}) }  \right] ^{-1}
\end{equation}

with balancing factors \(A_j\) and \(B_k\) calculated iteratively. The
model estimates commuter flows (\(T_{jk}\)) between origins (\(j\)) and
destinations (\(k\)), with constraints on the number of journeys at
each. \(f(d_{jk})\) is a spatial deterrence or decaying function. The
inverse power (\(d_{jk}^{-\beta}\)) and negative exponential
(\(e^{(-\beta d_{jk})}\)) decaying functions were tested. \(\beta\)
represents the deterrence parameter.

A modified specification intended to capture an add-up cost for
inter-zone journeys or analogously a benefit of residing and working
within the same zone is considered (Thorsen and Gitlesen 1998). This
includes a parameter \(\mu\) which affects only the diagonal elements of
the OD matrix. The term is given by the following expression:

\begin{equation} 
  \mu \delta_{jk}
\end{equation}

where Kronecker delta takes the following value:

\begin{equation}
  \delta_{ij} \left\{
    \begin{array}{ll}
        0 & \quad \text{if   } j \neq k \\
        1 & \quad \text{if   } j = k \\
    \end{array} 
   \right.\\
\end{equation}

The simultaneous calibration of the model parameters \(\beta\) and
\(\mu\) is achieved via optimization by minimizing the standardized root
mean square error (SRMSE) using the \emph{Nelder-Mead's simplex
algorithm} as implemented in base \texttt{R} (R Core Team 2021).

The preliminary analyses shows that the negative exponential function
provided more accurate estimates compared to the inverse power. Also,
the use of the within-zone parameter \(\mu\) improves the estimates.
Therefore, the selected specification is the modified negative
exponential including \(\mu\). The parameters employed to estimate
accessibility are shown in Table \label{tab:access-pars}.

\subsection{Spatial analysis}\label{spatial-analysis}

Table \ref{tab:sas-summary} summarises descriptive statistics for both
accessibility measures across various SAS. The mean values for the
measure considering all type of employment range from 59,400 in the 0.5
km grid to 107,000 in the postcode schemes, while the matching measure
ranges from 25,500 to 41,600. This indicates a smaller scale for the
latter due to restrictions imposed in employment opportunities. However,
the ratio between the means varies with scale. For instance, the `All'
measure is about 2.3 times larger in the 0.5 km grid and only 1.1 in the
4 km grid, with the postcode scheme at 2.6. Notably, the mean of the
matching measure increases steadily with scale, from 25,500 in the 0.5

consistent trend. The postcodes scheme results in the largest mean
values. This can be explained by zoning effects. Specifically,
peripheral regions, which are associated with low accessibility, tend to
be larger, thereby reducing the number of low-accessibility value units.
Meanwhile, equal-sized grids fragment these regions of the city into a
larger number of units, thereby pulling the mean lower.

\subsubsection{General versus matching accessibility and equity
implications}\label{general-versus-matching-accessibility-and-equity-implications}

In the next step of the analysis, the accessibility measures are
directly compared using a similarity rate, where values close to 0
indicate low similarity and values close to 1 indicate high similarity.
We are also interested in whether the differences between matching and
generic accessibility measures follow a spatial pattern. Figure
\ref{fig:lisa-map} displays the local Moran's \emph{I} clustering
results for the similarity rate across each SAS. High- and
mid-resolution SAS reveal a consistent West-East divide, with High-High
clusters located towards the West and Low-Low clusters towards the East,
using the CBD as a reference point. This suggests that differences
between the accessibility measures tend to be stronger in the eastern
areas of the city.

While the postcode and 4 km grid show a similar trend for Low-Low
clusters, the High-High patterns are less consistent across SAS. In
particular, the postcode SAS show substantial differences in the spatial
distribution of High-High clusters, with many located in peripheral
areas. Additionally, postcode zones in the periphery tend to be large,
regarding nearby employment opportunities more important, as travel
times to jobs outside the zone are generally long. Since the matching
measure accounts for all within-zone employment opportunities, the
similarity rate is higher in many peripheral zones. Thus, this is one
way in which zoning can interact in the differences between measures.

The overall West-East divide revealed in the LISA analysis is generally
consistent with well-known spatial trends of key socioeconomic
indicators in GMC, i.e.~with socio-economically advantaged population
residing in the West (\textbf{Ortega-Avila2023?}). To examine potential
systematic differences, the LISA clusters are examined by their social
composition. Figure \ref{fig:lisa-composition} displays the proportion
of the population which were classed in High-High or Low-Low clusters by
education quintile for all SAS. This only includes significant clusters.
It is clear that the accessibility measures are very similar for the
vast majority of the most educated population across all SAS. By
contrast, most of the population in less educated groups fall within
Low-Low clusters.

Likely, this is related to the characteristics presented in the previous
paragraph, where periphery zones tend display high similarity rates
because of the areas of these zones is large. At the same time, these
areas populated by low-education groups to a good extent, particularly
to the North and North-East.

\subsection{Main public transport network
assessment}\label{main-public-transport-network-assessment}

In this section, the focus shifts from spatial variations to temporal
changes, aiming to capture the effects of public transport network
extensions. To better distinguish these changes, the spatial unit of
aggregation is kept fixed. Therefore, the following analysis uses only
the 0.5 km grid, as it is the smallest and most detailed SAS.

Figure \ref{fig:relative-change} provides an overview of the relative
average changes in the network weighted by the population across various
temporal stages, using the distinct accessibility measures. The baseline
is set to the `Before 2010' period. Generally, both measures show
consistent trends, with notable increases in accessibility during the
Stage 3 and Stage 7. These include the introduction of a 25-kilometre
metro line (METRO-12) connecting south-east zones to the MPTN, and a BRT
line (MB-L7) in a central area of the MPTN, respectively. Extensions in
other stages show minor average increases.

There are additional insights to draw from the results. First, the scale
of the ratio is smaller for the matching measure, with the gap narrowing
as the percentile increases. This relates to the different density
distributions of the measures, where the `Matching' measure is more
skewed than the `All' measure. Still, both measures show parallel trends
for most stages, with a few exceptions. For example, the `All' measure
consistently declines between Stage 1 (`Before 2010') and Stage 4 (`2013
to 2015') across all thresholds, while the `Matching' measure only
begins to reflect this trend after the 15th percentile, showing similar
trends by the 30th percentile.

Another distinction is that the `Matching' measure shows significant
improvement in Stage 5 (`2015 to 2016') for all thresholds, which is not
captured by the `All' measure. This improvement corresponds to the BRT
corridor MXB-LII, located in the periphery of the MPTN and connecting
key corridors in the northern areas of the city. This suggests that some
northern populations were previously considered well-served by the
generic measure, even though the employment opportunities did not match
their qualifications. This means that the opening of MXB-LII enabled
access to matching employment opportunities for those who already had
access to a minimum number of general opportunities.

The global population-weighted average in Figure
\ref{fig:relative-change} highlights large accessibility benefits
introduced in Stage 7 (2018-2019). However, the equity analysis results
in Figure \ref{fig:headcount-lineplot} show consistent negligible
impacts across all percentiles and measures in this stage. This is
because the MB-L7, implemented during this stage, is centrally located
within the MPTN and primarily serves to articulate existing network
corridors. As a result, the additional accessibility to employment was
concentrated in areas that already exceeded the examined thresholds.

Figure \ref{fig:equity-barplot} shows the net population taken out from
various accessibility thresholds by education quintile across the MPTN
stages. Between Stage 2 and Stage 4, the groups benefiting from the
infrastructure are concentrated in the intermediate quantiles, a trend
more pronounced in the general accessibility measure. The matching
measure remains consistent for some stages, particularly for the 30th
percentile threshold. Interestingly, the matching measure reveals a
progressive distributional effect in Stage 5, where the benefits go to
lower-educated groups, between the first and third quintile. This makes
sense, since the MXB-LIII enabled direct connection to the MPTN to two
densely populated municipalities characterised by high-deprivation
levels, i.e.~\emph{Ciudad Nezahualcóyotl} and \emph{Chimalhuacan}.
However, these effects diminish at higher accessibility thresholds,
likely because low-educated populations reside in less central areas.
Thus, as the threshold increases, lower-educated zones fall short, while
areas with higher education levels are considered beneficiaries.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The results examining the spatial differences between generic and
matching accessibility measures showed important interactions with the
MAUP. Specifically, the larger the scale, the less relevant the
distinction of a matching accessibility is, as shown by the increased
correlation. To an extent, this is explained by the tendency of larger
units to emphasize within-zone opportunities, given the increased travel
costs for residents to leave the unit. Thus, at large scales,
accessibility measures tend to reflect employment density.
Interestingly, the post code retained matching employment information to
a very good extent, as shown by the low correlation. By implication and
as shown visually, the role of the public transport infrastructure is
gradually lost with the use of large scales too. Similarly, post code
units showed to retain some of these features. Analysts who aim to
account for matching characteristics and the role of transport
infrastructure should take these effects into consideration.

Furthermore, our results indicate that zones that showed significant
similarity between the `All' and `Matching' measures were predominantly
composed of the most educated population. This reflects a West-East
divide, aligning with the well-documented spatial inequalities in GMC,
e.g.~related to household income and car ownership
(\textbf{Guerra2015?}), healthy food environments
(\textbf{Ortega-Avila2023?}), and social mix (\textbf{Letouze2022?}).
From the urban economic perspective, high-educated populations are
likely to be in a position to afford residential locations that maximise
accessibility to employment opportunities that they consider valuable,
outbidding less affluent groups (\textbf{Duranton2015?}). An implication
for accessibility analyses which draw on the assumption of equivalent
attractiveness of employment might systematically overestimate the
relevant opportunities available to less educated populations. This is
expected to be more relevant in context with higher economic
inequalities.

The results regarding the evaluation of public transport infrastructure
over time are varied. Firstly, it is confirmed that a simple measure
alone, such as the global average of accessibility, might disregard key
transport equity insights (\textbf{Karner2024?}). Specifically, while
certain extensions of the MPTN reflect a substantial improvement on
average, an equity analysis from a sufficiency perspective reflects
negligible effects. We argue both indicators supplement each other. For
instance, the results highlight a concentration of the benefits in Stage
7 among populations that were already served. This is consistent
regardless of the accessibility measure being used.

Secondly, it is shown that at a higher level both accessibility measures
point into similar directions with parallel trends. However, there are
particularities to note. The matching accessibility measure was more
sensitive to sufficiency thresholds in the equity analysis. This might
be related to the observation that the distribution of the baseline to
define the thresholds presents a more skewed distribution.

The comparison between measures also showed some complementarity. For
example, the fact that the matching measure reflects that there are
populations benefited by particular infrastructure interventions and the
general does not, implies that certain populations might have received
`general' accessibility improvements previously and, later, specific
ones about matching employment opportunities. Both indicators are useful
when considering that the former measure as a form of \emph{generalized
access to urban opportunities}, as the location of employment often
reflects the provision of a service (\textbf{Merlin2017?}; Levinson and
Wu 2020). Meanwhile, a distinction of matching employment opportunities
is useful when it is applied to commuting to work patters or labour
market inequalities, for example.

Thirdly, the particular differences arising from the inclusion of
matching opportunities in equity analyses vary with a range of outcomes
from case-to-case. It is shown that the implementation of a transport
project can potentially imply benefits either for well educated or
low-educated groups only, or measures can be consistent at times. In the
area of study, this is observed to be related to the location of the
transport improvement with respect of the overall transport network, and
the demographic characteristics of the population in proximity to the
public transport services. This reinforces the important role of
accessibility measure in public transport evaluation, given that it is
able to capture key elements of the transport system and characteristics
of the population simultaneously. Thus, it is recommended to use both
measures to form a comprehensive insights of transport infrastructure on
a case-based basis.

\subsection{Limitations and future
work}\label{limitations-and-future-work}

The present study comes with the following implications. Specifically,
the analyses assume that all population within-zones share similar
demographic characteristics, e.g.~same education levels or employment
opportunities. This is a broader issue in geography discussed under the
`Ecological Fallacy' literature (\textbf{Walker2021?}). This is also
related to limitations of data, given that individual-level data for
both jobs and residents are restricted to maintain privacy. Also, the
modelled matching access measure used assume limited social mobility.
Namely, only equal education-income deciles are allowed. This can be
partially validated by verifying general trends in the area of study.
Still, additional research in different context can clarify the
sensitivity of the assumption. Furthermore, the analyses focus on the
MPTN only. This overlooks the street transit services. In the case of
study, this is largely constrained by the information available, given
that comprehensive structured timetable data is not available.

Future work is recommended to examine the sensitivity of results when
using individual-level job characteristics to establish a match between
residents and opportunities. Similar analysis in a diversity of context
with a range of social and economic disparities would help to identify
consistent patterns. This could help to establish the extent of the
findings in our empirical case. Furthermore, further work should examine
the assumptions of matching measures and their relevance for specific
applications, e.g.~in the housing market or commuting to work patterns.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

The present study relaxed a common assumption of accessibility analysis
that access to employment is equally attractive to all types of
residents. The study examines the specific differences between a generic
and matching accessibility measures taking into the account the MAUP,
and its relevance for equity assessments of public transport
infrastructure.

It is shown that both components of the modifiable areal unit problem,
namely scale and zoning, play an important role in this distinction.
Specifically, the added information regarding the matching employment is
gradually smoothed with increased scale. Also, the difference between
measures is sensitive to zoning as post codes, for example, can
over-represent the role or large zones in local spatial analyses.

A sensitivity analysis is recommended particularly in context with high
social inequality. This is because the allocation of residential land
use might push low-income residents more acutely with the consequence of
the level of access to relevant employment being systematically
underestimated for less advantaged populations. Also, the interpretation
and information provided by the distinct measures will depend on
specific applications. In this study, we have shown that both measures
play complementary roles.
