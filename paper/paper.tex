\begin{abstract}
The definition of accessibility encompasses the role of opportunities at
potential destinations that people consider valuable. This study revises
the common assumption in empirical studies that residents are equally
attracted to all types of employment and examines its implications for
public transport evaluation from a social equity perspective.
Additionally, the role of the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) is
also explored in this relationship. The study draws on the case of
Greater Mexico City over a ten-year period, in which seven temporal
stages of the main public transport network are examined. The key
results highlight a significant difference between accessibility
measures that account for employment matching and those that do not,
though these distinctions diminish when lower spatial resolutions are
used. The spatial analysis also shows that the differences are
consistently larger for lower-educated populations. In terms of public
transport infrastructure evaluation over time, the study confirms that
relying on simple measures, such as the global average, may overlook
critical transport equity insights. Additionally, the impact of
including employment matching in equity analyses varies, with outcomes
differing case by case. Depending on the accessibility measure, the
analyses show that a transport improvement might have progressive
effects with one measure, while another measure may indicate regressive
effects, or both measures can sometimes align. Overall, the comparisons
between measures suggest their complementarity in equity evaluations.
The findings have implications for researchers and policy analysts,
given the systematic differences in how transport projects tend to
affect less-educated populations and the heterogeneity in the type of
population impacted by specific transport projects on a case-by-case
basis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

A key function of a transport system is to provide people with access to
different locations so that they can physically participate in a diverse
range of activities (Miller 2018). Accessibility therefore has an
important role to play in transport planning, and various measures have
been proposed (see Geurs and van Wee (2023) for a summary).
Location-based measures, which indicate the level of accessibility to a
particular type of activity available at a location, have been
especially popular. They have been used to study the impacts of
accessibility on property prices (Osland and Thorsen 2008), labour
market outcomes (Johnson, Ercolani, and Mackie 2017), and social equity
(Tiznado-Aitken, Muñoz, and Hurtubia 2021), among other things.

A common application of the measures is to quantify accessibility to
employment. Measuring a location's access to jobs can incorporate a wide
range of factors. A simple measure might take into account only the
location of employment opportunities and a measure of impedance (e.g.,
travel time) incurred to reach those destinations from a given origin.
One assumption implicit in such a simple measure, and one common in
empirical studies, is that all types of employment are potentially
relevant for, and equally attractive to, everyone. Studies examining the
implications of this assumption are rare; See Osland (2010) or
Thériault, Voisin, and Des Rosiers (2013) for examples.

This paper makes an original contribution by examining the implications
of using a refined accessibility measure which reconsiders the
assumption of equally attractive employment opportunities. Drawing on
accessibility literature and the Human Capital Theory --- which suggests
that individuals invest in education and training to increase their
productivity and earnings potential (Becker 1994; Schultz 1961)--- we
categorise workers and jobs according to their respective education and
income levels, each of which is divided into quintiles. Workers only
match with a job if their level of education corresponds to the job's
level of income. We examine the implications of this refinement on
spatial patterns of accessibility and on the social distribution of
accessibility. Additional novelty is added by considering how the
modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) might impact in the way
accessibility is calculated. Accordingly, we address three research
questions:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  What are the key spatial differences between generic and matching
  accessibility measures and what is the role of the MAUP?
\item
  How does the evaluation of public transport infrastructure differ when
  accounting for matching employment opportunities in accessibility
  measures?
\item
  What are the social equity implications of the above?
\end{enumerate}

Our study is set in Greater Mexico City (GMC), an understudied
mega-city. We examine the impacts of a number of expansions of the main
public transport network (MPTN) over the period 2009 to 2019. In
general, we find that accounting for heterogeneity among workers and
jobs can have important implications for the conclusions we draw about
how changes to the transport network alter patterns of accessibility and
accessibility poverty. Our analysis of MAUP showed that larger areal
units tend to minimise the difference in accessibility measures
accounting for heterogeneity and those that ignore it. This is partly
because the longer travel times required to reach neighbouring areas
when using larger units reduce the attractiveness of employment
opportunities outside the zone of origin. Thus, larger areal units tend
to reflect employment density more closely, making the matching and
generic measures more similar. However, zoning effects can lead to some
of the differences being maintained even when moving to larger units.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section
\ref{sec:lit_review} presents a review of the relevant literature.
Section \ref{sec:methods} outlines the data and methods used in the
study. Our findings are presented in Section \ref{sec:findings}. Lastly,
the discussion of results and concluding remarks are offered in Section
\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Literature review
\label{sec:lit_review}}{Literature review }}\label{literature-review}

Levinson and Wu (2020) assert that accessibility measures ``the ease of
reaching valued destinations'' (p.~130). This notion combines two
fundamental elements, namely the ease of travel and the spatial
dispersion of opportunities reflected in land use patterns, including
services and other people, for example. Furthermore, this definition
limits opportunities to those which are deemed valuable. Thus, the
\emph{for whom} question becomes relevant. In line with this discussion,
Thériault, Voisin, and Des Rosiers (2013) provides a more explicit
definition, regarding it as ``the ability that persons living in that
area can, within an acceptable travel time, reach a satisfactory
diversity of activity locations that are important to them'' (p.~234).

The operationalisation of accessibility has taken various forms in the
literature (Geurs and van Wee 2004). Infrastructure-based measures focus
on the performance of the transport network, such as travel times or
distances. Person-based measures consider individual constraints, such
as time availability or personal mobility. Utility-based measures,
rooted in economic theory, estimate the perceived benefit or
satisfaction individuals derive from accessing opportunities.
Place-based measures are frequently used in empirical studies due to
their consistency with conceptual definitions, flexibility, and ease of
interpretation, enhancing their impact on transport policy. These
measures characterise space based on mobility constraints or enablers,
combined with land use patterns, by dividing regions into spatial units
or zones. This review focuses on location-based measures due to their
relevance in policy discussions and their ability to account for
people's abilities and preferences (Páez, Scott, and Morency 2012).

\subsection{Accessibility measures and empirical
application}\label{accessibility-measures-and-empirical-application}

Location-based measures consist of the sum of the product of two
functions, namely \(g(\cdot)\) and \(f(\cdot)\), as shown in Equation
\ref{eq:location-based1} (Páez, Scott, and Morency 2012):

\begin{equation}
   A^p_{ik} =  \sum_j g(W_{jk}) f(c^p_{ij}).
  \label{eq:location-based1}
\end{equation}

Here, \(A\) denotes the accessibility to opportunities of type \(k\)
from location \(i\), from the perspective of person type \(p\). The
measure reflects the attractiveness of opportunities \(W\) of type \(k\)
at destination \(j\). The term \(c_{ij}\) refers to a function of travel
cost between \(i\) and \(j\), as experienced by person \(p\).

Equation \ref{eq:location-based1} is often used in the literature to
measure accessibility to employment as follows:

\begin{equation}
  A_{i} = \sum^N_{i=1} E_j f(c_{ij})
  \label{eq:access-employment-general}
\end{equation}

The attractiveness of a destination \(W\) is expressed by \(E\), usually
as the number of employment opportunities at \(j\), i.e.~\(E_j\). Travel
cost in \(f(c_{ij})\) is often represented by travel time.

In Equation \ref{eq:access-employment-general}, households and
opportunities are implicitly assumed to be homogeneous, i.e., all types
of employment are equally attractive for all types of residents (Houston
2005; Shen 1998). For instance, a public transport equity study in
Chicago estimated accessibility to various destinations, including
employment, and analysed its association with socio-demographic
composition (Ermagun and Tilahun 2020). A higher proportion of
low-income residents was negatively associated with employment
accessibility, which was measured using the total number of jobs
available at each destination, regardless of type. In Santiago, Chile, a
study incorporating observed GPS-based public transport travel times
found that municipalities with lower income levels had significantly
lower accessibility to all types of employment (Basso et al. 2020).

Contributions from theory highlight the characteristics of the demand
(residents at origins) and supply at potential destinations (Geurs and
van Wee 2004; Levinson and Wu 2020). Specifically, Levinson and Wu state
that ``different job categories represent different economic activities,
and different types of jobs generally require different skills and
education levels'' (2020, 147). This distinction is supported by Human
Capital Theory (Schultz 1961; Becker 1994), which suggests that
individuals invest in their education, skills, and training with the
expectation of improving their productivity and, consequently, their
earning potential. Therefore, accessibility to employment is not just a
matter of spatial proximity, but also of alignment between job seekers
and job opportunities.

Location-based measures can consider some of these distinctive elements.
For instance, Equation \ref{eq:location-based1} refers to the following
components: (1) individual's profiles; (2) how different profiles
perceive or experience the space, and; (3) constraints that may prevent
segments of the population to access some type of activities. Some of
these aspects have been found to be relevant in empirical studies. For
instance, gender in Osland (2010) or age and gender in Thériault,
Voisin, and Des Rosiers (2013) in the study of the property prices.

The operationalisation of measures accounting for employment preferences
or restrictions can vary. In empirical studies, these characteristics
can be expressed as type of person \(m\) at \(j\) and type of employment
\(m\) at \(k\) (Pereira et al. 2019; Shen 1998; Pan, Jin, and Liu 2020).
Accordingly, only employment opportunities \(E\) matching the profile of
residents between \(j\) and \(k\) are considered in the accessibility
measure. This reflects the notion that residents would only find
attractive employment opportunities that match their skills or education
level.

In Pereira et al. (2019), an accessibility measure recognising such
characteristics is implemented as a \emph{proxy} match by distinguishing
the upper and lower halves of residents' income and education
requirements in employment opportunities. Specifically, the lower half
is matched only with employment opportunities requiring secondary or
primary education, while the upper half considers those requiring higher
or secondary education. Although the aim of this study is the
identification of potential inequalities in transport policy and not the
explicit discussion of this measure, the authors report no substantial
difference in their conclusions when disregarding the match in
accessibility measures.

Levinson and Wu (2020) suggest weighting employment in each category
with the number of eligible workers within each corresponding segment.
This perspective thus considers two aspects discussed in employment
mismatch literature, competition for employment opportunities and labour
segmentation. Bian (2020) refers to these aspects as a numerical
(objective) imbalance and an expectation imbalance (subjective),
respectively.

An empirical application which explores both numerical and expectation
imbalance can be found in Pan, Jin, and Liu (2020). To compute
accessibility measures in Houston, the study categorises employment
opportunities by the number of white- and blue-collar jobs at potential
destinations. On the residents' side, it considers unemployed
individuals as job seekers, categorising them by education level:
\emph{associate's degree or above} and \emph{high school degree or
lower}. White-collar jobs are assumed to be relevant only to job seekers
with an associate's degree or higher, while blue-collar jobs are
relevant to the less educated group. The measures also account for
different transport modes, i.e., automobile, drive to transit, and walk
to transit. Results show that accessibility is concentrated in core
areas, declining slowly for drivers and much more sharply for transit
users. The study concludes low-educated groups are more dependent of car
since blue-collar jobs are more dispersed across the city than
white-collar jobs.

Pan, Jin, and Liu (2020) follow a `descriptive' approach which difficult
its use in transport policy evaluation. This is because it focuses on
capturing the variability of accessibility among various population
groups within the same location, rather than summarising accessibility
into a single, aggregate measure for each zone. While this provides
valuable insights into intra-zonal differences, it complicates the
comparison between areas and the identification of priority zones for
policy intervention. Additionally, it does not address the differences
or implications of using education and employment matching compared to
accessibility measures that disregard these distinctions.

\subsubsection{The modifiable areal unit problem in accessibility
measures}\label{the-modifiable-areal-unit-problem-in-accessibility-measures}

Location-based measures tend to characterise space in discrete units.
Thus, these often suffer from the MAUP. The MAUP literature
distinguishes two main types of issues, namely \emph{scale} effects and
\emph{zoning} effects (Wong 2009b). Scale effects refer to the
variability in spatial analysis results depending on the spatial
resolution used. Zoning effects, on the other hand, arise from the
spatial configuration of areas while maintaining the same number of
zones in an area of study. Unless location-based accessibility is
calculated at the individual level, it will likely be affected by these
issues. Therefore, this is an important consideration for most
location-based applications.

Considerable effort has been made to understand the effects and
structural patterns of the MAUP. The clearest effects are observed in
simple univariate statistics. It has been noted that while mean values
remain fairly constant across scales, variance tends to decline as
spatial resolution decreases, with a narrower range at lower resolutions
(Dark and Bram 2007; Wong 2009a). However, this structure tends to
dissipate in multivariate regression analyses (Fotheringham and Wong
1991). Zoning effects, on the other hand, are less predictable and are
influenced by factors such as spatial autocorrelation and aggregation
methods, e.g., mean, sum, median (Wong 2009a). Zoning effects are
minimal in cases of random spatial patterns, but results are highly
sensitive to zoning when strong positive spatial autocorrelation exists
(Lee et al. 2019).

Accessibility studies often use a single unit of analysis. Thus,
literature is limited in the role of MAUP in location-accessibility
(Stepniak and Rosik 2015). An empirical study in Poland examined three
gravity-type accessibility measures at different spatial scales. The
findings showed that larger units, such as municipalities, produced
smoother estimates compared to disaggregated grid-based inputs,
consistent with MAUP literature (Wong 2009a). Additionally, differences
in self-potential estimates were attributed to the gravity measures'
components (e.g., transport and land use) rather than the estimation
process itself. Further work has suggested that errors arise from
aggregating travel times within zones (intra-zone) and between zones
(inter-zone) (Stepniak and Jacobs-Crisioni 2017). A comparison with
disaggregated travel times suggests that using population-weighted
centroids provides more accurate results than geometric centroids or
areal-based methods Stepniak and Rosik (2015). Pereira, Grégoire, and
Karner (2019) investigated the equity effects of transport
infrastructure in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, taking MAUP into account. The
study conducted three analyses that utilised two hexagonal grids and
traffic zones. The findings indicate that while the direction of the
relationship between accessibility increments and residents' income
remained stable, there were significant differences in the magnitude and
significance of this association across different spatial aggregations.

Although there are recent and relevant contributions considering MAUP
and location-based accessibility measures, there are no studies
examining the interactions with refined accessibility measures, e.g.,
measures considering matching opportunities.

\subsection{Equity evaluation of transport in transport
infrastructure}\label{equity-evaluation-of-transport-in-transport-infrastructure}

Transport equity concerns the adequate allocation of transport resources
among different population groups and places (Lucas et al. 2019). This
issue can be framed within the context of social justice, which is often
defined as the ``morally proper distribution of benefits and burdens
among members of a society'' (Lucas et al. 2019, 7). This definition
captures the essence of transport equity by incorporating three key
elements (ibid): 1) the resource being distributed, such as mobility or
accessibility; 2) the social attributes of society's members; and 3) the
moral principles used to assess whether a given allocation is fair or
equitable (Martens, Bastiaanssen, and Lucas 2019).

The `equity standard', implicit in the third point, is major topic of
discussion in literature. The typical questions involve, for example,
should everyone receive the same amount of the good or carry the same
burden, should populations in most need receive the most, etc. Karner,
Pereira, and Farber (2024) discuss two main moral philosophies
addressing these questions, namely the egalitarian and the
sufficientarian. The former addresses the systematic exclusion or
reduction of opportunities for neglected populations. The latter is
about ensuring that minimal resources are allocated in a way that allows
people to meet essential needs.

Location-based accessibility measures have been used as a key benefit
being distributed in the evaluation of the performance of public
transport infrastructure in policy reports and research (Ermagun 2021;
L. Liu, Porr, and Miller 2022; Conway, Byrd, and Van Eggermond 2018;
Malekzadeh and Chung 2020; Palmateer et al. 2021). One motivation is
because the use of realistic travel times can reflect important elements
of a public transport system, such as the topology of the network,
spatial network, and operational characteristics.

In line with its philosophical and moral perspective, the
operationalisation of the accessibility poverty notion requires the
definition of a sufficiency threshold. Accessibility poverty draws
inspiration from the income poverty literature and adopts an analogous
poverty line (Martens and Bastiaanssen 2019). For analytical purposes,
studies often utilise a percentile of the number of accessible
opportunities. For example, Karner, Pereira, and Farber (2024) employs
the population-weighted median of job accessibility in the Washington
metropolitan area, i.e.~50th percentile. Similarly, Martens and
Bastiaanssen (2019) introduces a combined index that incorporates the
percentile of employment accessible by car alongside income deprivation,
indicating that an individual or group is considered to be in
accessibility poverty if they face both low public transport
accessibility and income deprivation. This approach is founded on the
understanding that marginalised populations rely on public transport,
and low-income households may either be unable to afford the costs
associated with maintaining a car or find it undesirable to do so.

Despite the growing relevance of accessibility measures in the
evaluation of public transport infrastructure, knowledge of the
implications of overlooking matching employment are limited in the
literature. In this study, we aim to examine the implications of the use
of a matching accessibility measure accounting for the MAUP, and its
role in transport infrastructure equity evaluation. We cover a 10-year
period on a empirical study which includes a range of transport
projects.

\subsection{Study area and public transport
network}\label{study-area-and-public-transport-network}

Greater Mexico City (GMC) (\emph{Zona Metropolitana del Valle de
México}) is located in the south-centre of Mexico. In 2015, GMC had
nearly 21 million inhabitants (INEGI 2015b). The official delimitation
consists of 76 adjacent municipalities, which are part of three
different states, namely: (1) \emph{Ciudad de México} (16
municipalities); (2) \emph{Hidalgo} (1 municipality), and (3)
\emph{México} (59 municipalities), as shown in Figure
\ref{fig:metro-map}. The total GMC area is 7,889 km\textsuperscript{2},
and it is home to 17\% of the national population.

\subsubsection{The main public transport network
(MPTN)}\label{the-main-public-transport-network-mptn}

The focus of this work is \emph{rapid transit} and \emph{semi-rapid
transit} infrastructure, excluding specialized modes i.e., \emph{aerial
tramway}. The combination of public transport modes included in the
analyses is referred to as the \emph{main public transport network}
(MPTN). Figure \ref{fig:metro-map} shows the MPTN in GMC in the year
2019. This consists of a variety of modes operated by the following main
agencies: The metro system, directly operated by the state-owned company
\emph{Sistema de Transporte Colectivo} (STC) and designated as Metro;
two BRT systems, namely \emph{Metrobus} (MB) in Ciudad de México and
Mexibus (MXB) in the state of México; One LRT line operated by
\emph{Sistema de Transporte Masivo} (STE); and one regional rail line
designated as \emph{Sub}.

The period of study was divided into seven temporal stages to reflect
significant changes in the MPTN over time. Initially, changes were
identified by the physical alterations in the MPTN, such as the opening
of a new corridor or the extension of an existing one. Table

time interval between changes, with modifications occurring within six
months being grouped together in the same stage. This criteria follows

perceived as simultaneous when changes happen within short timeframes
(Ahlfeldt 2013; Gibbons and Machin 2005).

\subsection{Data}\label{data}

\subsubsection{Demographic}\label{demographic}

The demographic information comes from the 2010 Population Census (INEGI
2012). These data were selected for their detailed disaggregation at the
city block level, including key fields such as total population and
average years of education completed by individuals aged 15 and older.

The count data included were transferred to the SAS using the
\emph{areal weighting method} (Saporito et al. 2007). This reassigns the
data according to the proportional overlay of the source and target
areas. The average education level was assigned using the
\emph{population weighting method}. This takes into consideration the
number of inhabitants and their type. This is visualised in the
\hyperref[appendix]{Appendix}.

\subsubsection{Employment}\label{employment}

Employment data are sourced from the 2014 Economic Census (\emph{Censos
Económicos 2014}) (INEGI 2015b) and accessed at the city block level via
the Microdata Lab and Remote Processing service
(\url{https://www.inegi.org.mx/microdatos/}). The dataset includes: 1.
Number of establishments (fixed locations producing goods or services);
2. Employed personnel (individuals active in establishments, including
volunteers and family members), referred to as `employment'; and 3.
Total gross remuneration paid for the relevant year.

Employment information, which came aggregated at a higher geographic
level than the city-block due to privacy concerns (\textless8\%), were
re-allocated using proportional estimates based on the spatial
distribution from the 2015 DENUE (INEGI 2015a). This is an open-access,
point-level dataset of individual establishments.

The employment data was also assigned from city blocks to the SAS
following the areal weighting method (Saporito et al. 2007). The average
income was computed for each areal unit based on the total number of
employment positions and the total gross remuneration paid. The spatial
distribution of across various SAS is shown in the
\hyperref[appendix]{Appendix}.

\subsubsection{Public transport
timetables}\label{public-transport-timetables}

The estimated travel time used in the accessibility measures is based on
scheduled public transport services. The Transport Authority in Mexico
City (\emph{Secretaría de Movilidad de la Ciudad de México}, Semovi)
released the first \emph{general transit feed specification} (GTFS)
files in 2013, which were accessed via the Open Mobility website
(\url{https://openmobilitydata.org/}). These feeds cover the agencies
operating most MPTN of the services in GMC with approximate annual
updates. The geographic coverage of the official GTFS data was
supplemented to include three BRT corridors operated by MXB. This was
done in \texttt{R} programming environment following the best practices
for structuring GTFS from \url{http://gtfs.org/}. Additional details are
available in the \hyperref[appendix]{Appendix}.

The MPTN's temporal stages were reconstructed using the GTFS standard.
The primary input for each stage was the closest available official GTFS
data reflecting changes in the MPTN, with only the relevant services for
each corridor in their respective stages being retained. For MXB
services, 2019 data was considered due to the lack of earlier details,
assuming uniform characteristics of the BRT corridors across the stages.
Similarly, for other corridors included in early stages, services were
assumed to reflect 2013 levels due to the unavailability of earlier
data. All non-MPTN services were excluded, thereby focusing exclusively
on the benefits of the MPTN.

\subsubsection{Road network and pedestrian
infrastructure}\label{road-network-and-pedestrian-infrastructure}

The road and pedestrian infrastructure network is sourced from
\emph{OpenStreetMap} (OSM) (Manually downloaded on 30th October 2019
through a web export tool available in
\url{https://export.hotosm.org/es/v3/}{]}). This enables multi-modal
journeys to realistically represent the access/egress stage to a public
transport service when combining walking and public transport.

\subsection{Modelling travel time}\label{modelling-travel-time}

Travel time by public transport was calculated for each
origin-destination (OD) pair across each SAS using a combination of
walking and public transport services within the main public transport
network. OD points are represented by population-weighted centroids of
spatial units. Travel times are capped at 180 minutes, based on patterns
observed in the 2017 Travel Survey (INEGI 2018). Travel times were
modelled using a door-to-door approach with the \texttt{r5r} software
package for the \texttt{R} programming language (Saraiva et al. 2021; R
Core Team 2021). This approach considers access, waiting, onboard,
transfer, and egress times. All-to-all travel time matrices were
generated for each SAS across seven temporal stages using reconstructed
GTFS files and road/pedestrian networks. The estimated travel time
considers the median time within a three-hour time window between 8 a.m.
and 11 a.m. The maximum walking distance to access and egress public
transport or in walking journeys only is limited to 40 minutes,
approximately 3.2 km at an average walking speed of 4.8 km/h.

Travel time by private car is modelled using OpenTripPlanner. The
initial estimate is based on free-flow model. These estimates were then
adjusted to account for congestion and other unobserved factors using
travel times obtained from
\href{https://developers.google.com/apis-explorer}{Google API} services
for a sample of 9,264 randomly selected OD pairs. This adjustment
reflects typical conditions on a departure time at 10 a.m. on a
Wednesday. The difference between the routing estimates was modelled
using a linear regression framework, and the resulting model was applied
to the remaining OD pairs. Additionally, an 8.1-minute parking search
time was considered, following literature and consistent with the
door-to-door approach (Salonen and Toivonen 2013; Shoup 2006).

The internal-zone travel-times were estimated based on the surface area
using: \(d_{j=k} = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{ \frac{area_j}{\pi}}\) (Frost and
Spence 1995; Stepniak and Rosik 2015). The metric distance was
transformed to travel time using the average speed modelled for short
journeys (less than 3 km).

\subsection{Analytical framework}\label{analytical-framework}

\subsubsection{Accessibility measures
examined}\label{accessibility-measures-examined}

For this study, a general accessibility measure---referred to as
`All'---is computed using the following below. This label is defined by
the authors for consistency within the analysis and is not drawn from
existing classifications in the literature.

\begin{equation}
  A^{All}_{jt} = \sum^J_{j=1} E_k f(c^{PT}_{jkt}),
   \label{eq:empirical-access-general}
\end{equation}

where \(A^{All}_{jt}\) denotes the general accessibility to employment
from origin \(j\) at time \(t\). \(E_k\) are the employment
opportunities at potential destinations, and \(c^{PT}_{jkt}\) is the
travel time by the MPTN from \(j\) to \(k\) at temporal stage \(t\).

For this work, the implementation of the matching accessibility measure
is adapted from Pereira et al. (2019) and Pan, Jin, and Liu (2020) as
follows:

\begin{equation}
  A^{Matching}_{jtm} = \sum^J_{j=1} E_{km} f(c^{PT}_{jkt}).
   \label{eq:empirical-access-matching}
\end{equation}

Here, \(A^{Matching}_{jtm}\) is the matching accessibility measure from
\(j\), at temporal stage \(t\), for opportunities of type \(m\). \(m\)
at \(j\) is categorised in education quintiles based on the median
number of years in formal education using residents' information from
the population census (originally sourced at the city block level as
detailed in Section 3.2). \(m\) at \(k\) is based on the average salary
paid at destinations classified by quintiles as well. Only employment
opportunities \(E\) matching the same education quantile between \(j\)
and \(k\) are considered in this measure. This reflects that residents
would deem valuable only employment opportunities that match their
education level.

The key adaptation of the measure compared to the references in
empirical studies consists in accounting for education level at the
origin (characterising residents), rather than residents' income.
Additionally, job opportunities are characterised by the salaries paid,
rather than by job type at the destination. This variation reflects the
data availability and it is supported by the positive relationship
between education level and wages in the study area (Nuñez, Paredes, and
Garduño-Rivera 2017; Rojas, Angulo, and Velázquez 2000). Additionally,
this is motivated by the data available in the area of study, as
individual income information is not available from the 2010 Population
Census at the block level.

\subsubsection{Spatial decay function and calibration of accessibility
parameters}\label{spatial-decay-function-and-calibration-of-accessibility-parameters}

The spatial decay function and parameters for the accessibility measures
were determined using a trip distribution model, following Handy and
Niemeier (1997). A trip distribution model estimates how many trips
occur between different origin-destination pairs within a region based
on the attractiveness of destinations and the spatial separation between
them. In this context, the spatial decay parameter \(\beta\) reflects
how individuals perceive the spatial separation between where they live
and potential destinations. The estimation details are available in the
\hyperref[appendix]{Appendix C}.

The selected decay function used in the accessibility measures in
Equation \ref{eq:empirical-access-general} and Equation
\ref{eq:empirical-access-matching} is the modified negative exponential
including \(\mu\), as shown below:

\begin{equation} 
  f(c_{jkt}) = e^{(-\beta c^{PT}_{jkt} + \mu \delta_{jk})}
\label{eq:seleected-decaying-function}
\end{equation}

where, \(c^{PT}_{jkt}\) is the modelled travel time from \(j\) to \(k\)
at the MPTN temporal stage \(t\).

The optimized parameters used are \(\beta = 0.044\) and \(\mu = 1.654\).

\subsubsection{Accessibility indicators}\label{accessibility-indicators}

To focus on the key spatial differences between generic and matching
accessibility measures and the role of the MAUP, the first section of
the analysis summarises the level accessibility over the period of study
as following:

\begin{equation} 
  A^{All}_{j} = \text{median}(A^{All}_{jt}) \text{ for } t= 1,2,..,7,
\label{eq:median-accessibility-all}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation} 
  A^{Matching}_{jm} = \text{median}(A^{Matching}_{jtm}) \text{ for } t= 1,2,..,7. 
\label{eq:median-accessibility-matching}
\end{equation}

A similarity rate is computed to compare the All and Matching measures
as following:

\begin{equation} 
  \text{Similarity rate} = \frac{  A^{Matching}_{jm} } { A^{All}_{j} }.
\label{eq:similarity-rate}
\end{equation}

This ranges between 0 and 1, where values near to 0 indicate low
similarity and 1 a high similarity rate.

\subsubsection{Accessibility equity
measures}\label{accessibility-equity-measures}

For the transport equity evaluation of public transport infrastructure
projects, the prevalence of accessibility shortfalls measure is adopted
from Martens and Bastiaanssen (2019). This is expressed by the equation
below:

\begin{equation}
  H_t = \sum^{P}_{j=1} I(A_{jt} < z)
\label{eq:accessibility-shortfalls}
\end{equation}

Where, \(H_t\) represents the number of people whose accessibility level
falls below the established accessibility sufficiency threshold at
temporal stage \(t\), \(P\) is the total population, \(A_{jt}\) is the
accessibility level of at location \(j\) and temporal stage \(t\), \(z\)
is the sufficiency threshold, and \(I(A_{it} < z)\) is an indicator
function that equals 1 if \(y_{it}\) is less than the sufficiency
threshold (\(z\)), and 0 otherwise. We also use the headcount ratio
\(P_0 = H_t / N\), where \(N\) represents the total population in \(i\).
Thus, a larger share indicates a less fair transport system. Given that
this is a standardised measure, we can compare the different temporal
stages.

The measure adopted aligns with Martens and Bastiaanssen (2019)'s
broader perspective, which acknowledges that low-income populations
often cannot afford or maintain a car and therefore rely more heavily on
public transport. This is supported by empirical evidence from Mexico
and North America (Allen et al. 2023; Guerra et al. 2018; D. Liu and
Kwan 2020).

This study conducts a sensitivity analysis using a range of sufficiency
thresholds for \(z\) commonly found in empirical studies, namely the 10,
15, 20, and 25 percentiles of accessibility by car for each
corresponding measure, i.e., All or Matching. Accessibility by car is
estimated using Equation \ref{eq:empirical-access-general} and Equation
\ref{eq:empirical-access-matching} where
\(f(c_{jk}) = \text{exp}(-\beta c^{Car}_{jk})\). \(c^{Car}_{jk}\) is the
estimated travel time by car. This approach draws on Martens and
Bastiaanssen (2019). For clarity, the main body of the paper presents
the results of the 15th percentile. The Appendix shows the full
findings.

Using car-based thresholds aligns with urban and transport equity views
(Soja 2010; Lucas 2012), which suggest that inequities arise when
certain groups systematically face lower access to opportunities
compared to what is considered the societal norm. Here, car travel
defines this norm due to its greater reliability, speed and coverage.

\subsubsection{Indicators of spatial
association}\label{indicators-of-spatial-association}

Two spatial statistics techniques are used to measure the spatial
difference between the All and Matching measures. The first, the Global
Moran's I statistic, which measures spatial autocorrelation, is
expressed as (Moraga 2023):

\begin{equation}
  I = \frac{N \sum_{j} \sum_{k} w_{jk} (x_j - \bar{x}) (x_k - \bar{x})}{\left( \sum_{j \neq k} w_{jk} \right) \sum_{j} (x_j - \bar{x})^2}
\label{eq:moran-i-statistic}
\end{equation}

Where, \(N\) is the number of individual units in each SAS, \(x_j\) and
\(x_k\) are the accessibility levels at location \(j\) and \(k\),
respectively, \(\bar{x}\) is the mean accessibility, \(w_{jk}\)
represents a binary spatial weight between locations \(i\) and \(j\),
and the term \(\sum^N_{j=1} \sum^N_{k=1}\) is the sum of the spatial
weights. \(w_{jk}\) is an element of the spatial weight matrix \(W\),
which was computed based on 6-nearest neighbours of the OD points with
the \texttt{spdep} package (Bivand 2021).

Local Moran's I is used to account for local indicators of spatial
association (LISA) on the similarity rate (defined in Equation
\ref{eq:similarity-rate}). Local Moran's I is computed as:

\begin{equation}
  I_j = \frac{N (x_j - \bar{x})}{\sum_{k} (x_k - \bar{x})^2} \sum_{k} w_{jk} (x_k - \bar{x})
\label{eq:lisa-statistic}
\end{equation}

A positive value for \(I_j\) suggests that area \(j\) is surrounded by
areas with similar values. Conversely, a negative value indicates that
area \(j\) is surrounded by dissimilar values. This helps identify
different types of spatial clusters, such as high-high or low-low
clusters (where areas with high or low values are surrounded by areas
with similarly high or low values), as well as spatial outliers, such as
high-low or low-high patterns (where an area with a high value is
surrounded by low-value neighbours, or vice versa).

\subsection{Main public transport network
assessment}\label{main-public-transport-network-assessment}

In this section, the focus shifts from spatial variations to temporal
changes, aiming to capture the effects of public transport network
extensions. To better distinguish these changes, the spatial unit of
aggregation is kept fixed. Therefore, the following analysis uses only
the 0.5 km grid, as it is the smallest and most detailed SAS.

Figure \ref{fig:relative-change} provides an overview of the relative
average changes in the network weighted by the population across various
temporal stages, using the distinct accessibility measures. The baseline
is set to the `Before 2010' period. Generally, both measures show
consistent trends, with notable increases in accessibility during Stage
3 and Stage 7. These include the introduction of a 25-kilometre metro
line (METRO-12) connecting south-east zones to the MPTN, and a BRT line
(MB-L7) in a central area of the MPTN, respectively. Extensions in other
stages show minor average increases.

  \caption{Relative change of accessibility population weighted mean over various temporal stages.}

\end{figure}

Interestingly, the general accessibility measure in Figure
\ref{fig:relative-change} shows moderate increases in Stage 4
(2013-2015) and Stage 5 (2015-2016), while the matching measure records
little to no change, and even a slight decline during these stages. This
divergence suggests that while the infrastructure improvements
introduced in these periods allowed access to additional employment from
certain residential zones, the jobs at destinations did not match the
educational qualifications in the areas of influence.

By taking the sufficiency accessibility threshold as a benchmark, we can
distinguish between populations that were already served at an
`adequate' level before a transport intervention occurs and those who
were not. Strictly, this is not a transport poverty measure as it
considers all populations. Figure \ref{fig:headcount-lineplot} shows the
head count ratio which are under various thresholds over the MPTN
temporal stages for the `All' and `Matching' measures. From the plot, a
general reduction in the population under the minimal thresholds can be
observed. This is in the direction expected.

There are additional insights to draw from the results. First, the scale
range of the ratio is smaller for the matching measure. Both measures
show parallel trends for most stages, with a few exceptions. For
example, the `All' measure consistently declines between Stage 1
(`Before 2010') and Stage 4 (`2013 to 2015'). Meanwhile, the `Matching'
measure decreases more steadily over these stages.

Another distinction is that the `Matching' measure shows significant
improvement in Stage 5 (`2015 to 2016'), which is not shown by the `All'
measure. This improvement corresponds to the BRT corridor MXB-LII,
located in the periphery of the MPTN and connecting key corridors in the
northern areas of the city. This suggests that some northern populations
were previously considered well-served by the `All' measure, even though
the employment opportunities did not match their qualifications. This
means that the opening of MXB-LII enabled access to matching employment
opportunities for those who already had access to a minimum number of
general opportunities.

The global population-weighted average shown in Figure
\ref{fig:relative-change} highlights large accessibility benefits
introduced in Stage 7 (2018-2019). However, the results in Figure
\ref{fig:headcount-lineplot} show consistent negligible impacts (this is
consistent across all percentiles and measures in this stage, as shown
in the sensitivity analysis in the Appendix). This is because the MB-L7,
implemented during this stage, is centrally located within the MPTN and
primarily serves to articulate existing network corridors. As a result,
the additional accessibility to employment was concentrated in areas
that already exceeded the sufficiency thresholds.

Figure \ref{fig:equity-barplot} shows the net population taken out from
the sufficiency threshold by education quintile across the MPTN stages.
A first observation is that values are often higher for the generic
measure than for the matching one, as seen in Stage 2 and Stage 3. This
reinforces the idea that although infrastructure enabled additional
accessibility to employment, these opportunities were not necessarily
relevant to the intended population. In other words, a generic measure
may overstate the benefits under certain conditions.

Another striking finding is that both measures show little to no
improvement for the lowest-educated populations over the period
examined. However, this comes with a few notable differences. For
instance, in Stage 4, the `All' measure indicates that over 200,000
people in the lowest quintile benefited, compared to fewer than 50,000
under the matching measure. Nonetheless, both measures align in showing
that infrastructure improvements mostly benefited lower-educated
populations (quintiles 1 to 3) in this particular stage.

Figure \ref{fig:equity-barplot} also highlights the matching employment
benefits in Stage 5 (2015-2016), as discussed earlier. Interestingly,
most of these improvements went to the more educated population between
the third and fifth quintiles. As noted, no significant population was
taken out of the sufficiency accessibility thresholds in Stages 6 and 7.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Conclusion and discussion
\label{sec:discussion}}{Conclusion and discussion }}\label{conclusion-and-discussion}

The results examining the spatial differences between generic and
matching accessibility measures showed important interactions with the
MAUP. Specifically, the larger the grid size, the less relevant the
distinction of matching accessibility becomes, as indicated by the
increased correlation. To some extent, this is explained by the tendency
of larger units to emphasise within-zone opportunities, given the
increased travel costs for residents to leave the unit or reach another
one. Interestingly, the postcode retained matching employment
information to a good extent. By implication, and as shown visually, the
role of public transport infrastructure gradually diminishes with larger
grid units. Similarly, postcode units demonstrated a retention of some
of these features. Analysts who aim to account for matching
characteristics and the role of transport infrastructure should take
these effects into consideration.

Furthermore, our results indicate that zones with high similarity
between the `All' and `Matching' measures were predominantly composed of
the most educated population. This adds to the existing evidence of the
east-west inequality divide in the GMC, beyond disparities related to
household income and car ownership (Guerra 2015), healthy food
environments (Ortega-Avila 2023), and social mix (Letouzé et al. 2022).
This finding can be discussed from an urban economic perspective.
Specifically, highly educated populations are likely to be in a position
to afford residential locations that maximise accessibility to
employment opportunities they consider valuable, outbidding less
affluent groups (Duranton and Puga 2015). An implication for
accessibility analyses that draw on the assumption of equivalent
attractiveness of employment is that they might systematically
overestimate the relevant opportunities available to less educated
populations. This is expected to be particularly relevant in contexts
with higher economic inequalities, following urban land-rent theory.

Studies examining the implications of employment matching in
accessibility measures are limited. Although not the main aim of Pereira
et al. (2019), it was reported that conclusions remained largely
unchanged when the worker--job match was disregarded. The contrasting
findings may relate to operationalisation. For instance, our matching
measure only considers matches within the same education--wage quintile,
whereas Pereira et al. (2019)'s study divided residential locations into
two income-based groups and matched them to jobs' educational
requirements, resulting in a more flexible approach. Further research
comparing alternative strategies could clarify the sensitivity to
methodological choices.

The results regarding the evaluation of public transport infrastructure
over time are varied. Firstly, it is confirmed that a simple measure
alone, such as the global average of accessibility, might overlook key
distributional insights (Karner, Pereira, and Farber 2024).
Specifically, while certain extensions of the MPTN reflect a substantial
improvement on average, an analysis from a sufficiency perspective shows
negligible effects, e.g.~in Stage 7 (2018 to 2019). In this case, the
mean and sufficiency threshold supplement each other. For instance,
these results highlight a concentration of the benefits in Stage 7 among
populations that were already well served. This finding was consistent
regardless of the accessibility measure being used.

Both accessibility measures point in similar directions with parallel
trends at a higher level. However, there are particularities to note.
The matching accessibility measure was more sensitive to sufficiency
thresholds. This may be related to the observation that the distribution
of the baseline used to define the thresholds presents a more skewed
distribution. Nonetheless, the comparison between measures also showed
some complementarity. For example, the fact that the matching measure
reflects that there are populations benefited by particular
infrastructure interventions, while the general measure does not,
implies that certain group might have received `general' accessibility
improvements previously and, later, specific ones related to matching
employment opportunities. Both indicators are useful when considering
the former measure as a form of \emph{generalized access to urban
opportunities}, as the location of employment often reflects the
provision of a service (Merlin 2017; Levinson and Wu 2020). Meanwhile, a
distinction of matching employment opportunities is useful when applied
to commuting to work patterns or labour market inequalities, for
example.

The particular differences arising from the inclusion of matching
opportunities in sufficiency analyses vary with a range of outcomes from
case to case. It is shown that the implementation of a transport project
can potentially imply benefits either for well-educated or low-educated
groups only, or that measures can be consistent at times. In the area of
study, this is observed to be related to the location of the transport
improvement with respect to the overall transport network and the
demographic characteristics of the population in proximity to the public
transport services. This reinforces the important role of accessibility
measures in public transport evaluation, given that they are able to
capture key elements of the transport system and characteristics of the
population simultaneously. Thus, it is recommended to use both measures
to form comprehensive insights into the impact of transport
infrastructure on a case-based basis. In regions lacking granular data,
reduction factors may be considered, as matched employment opportunities
are generally lower than those identified by generic measures, with
larger gaps observed among low-educated groups.

Finally, the results show that the least educated populations
experienced little to no improvement during the period studied. This
pattern is consistent across both accessibility measures, but matching
accessibility is particularly relevant in Stage 4. This aligns with
recent findings in London (Nie et al. 2024). This may reflect the
tendency of disadvantaged populations to be expelled to peripheral
areas. As a result, even when network extensions reach new
municipalities, they may still miss the most marginalised groups. These
results support the need for integrated transport--land use strategies
to ensure disadvantaged populations benefit from public investments. For
instance, inclusionary zoning requires or incentivises developers to
include a proportion of affordable housing units within residential
projects (Schuetz and Meltzer 2012). Such approaches could allow
low-income households to remain closer to employment opportunities that
match their skills and interests.

\subsection{Limitations and future
work}\label{limitations-and-future-work}

The present study comes with the following limitations. Specifically,
populations within zones are assumed to share similar characteristics,
such as education levels or employment opportunities. This is a broader
issue in geography discussed under the ecological fallacy literature
(Walker 2021). It is also related to data constraints, as
individual-level information for both jobs and residents is restricted
to maintain privacy. For the equity analyses, aggregation issues are
minimised by using the smallest available geographic unit (0.5 km grid).
While heterogeneity within these units may still be substantial in dense
metropolitan areas, this approach reduces the risk compared to coarser
units. Nevertheless, the potential impact of within-zone heterogeneity
cannot be fully ruled out. Future work could incorporate sensitivity
analyses leveraging finer-grained data where available.

Additionally, the modelled matching access measure used assumes limited
social mobility, allowing only equal education-income quintiles to be
matched. Additional research in different contexts can clarify the
sensitivity of this assumption. For example, future analyses could
incorporate cross-quantile mobility and account for potential sources of
heterogeneity, such as the requirement for high educational attainment
in low-wage entry-level positions if data is available.

Furthermore, the analyses focus on the MPTN only, which overlooks street
transit services. In the case of the study, this is largely constrained
by the information available, given that comprehensive structured
timetable data is not accessible. Also, the driving time estimates are
limited to a fixed scenario based on the 2019 OSM network. This may
introduce some inaccuracies due to potential changes in the road
infrastructure over the study period. However, the sensitivity analysis
using a range of accessibility percentiles as thresholds offers
robustness to the findings.

Future work is recommended to examine the sensitivity of results when
using individual-level job characteristics to establish a match between
residents and employment opportunities. This could include factors such
as the industry of the establishment or the number of jobs by
type---e.g., blue- or white-collar---available at destinations, as
collected in the Economic Census. Additionally, similar analyses in a
diversity of contexts with a range of social and economic disparities
would help to identify consistent patterns. Furthermore, additional work
should examine the assumptions of matching measures and their relevance
for specific applications, e.g.~in the housing market or commuting to
work patterns.

The temporal divergences among the data sources used are acknowledged,
i.e.~the demographic, economic, and transport data. This reflects a
common limitation in urban accessibility studies, as disaggregate
socio-demographic data are commonly available from decennial censuses.
Specifically, the operational information for three Mexibus corridors
(out of 24 in the MPTN) is extrapolated over time due to the lack of
detailed historical records, with periods ranging from 4 (MXB-LII) to 9
years (MXB-LI). Substantial operational changes during these periods
could lead to misrepresentation in the accessibility models. While this
remains a limitation, its impact is restricted to some geographies
(northern areas) and earlier stages.

The analysis intentionally excludes the COVID-19 period, which likely
incentivised changes in land use and mobility patterns. The authors are
not aware of any major political or economic shocks during the study
period that would have significantly altered land-use structures or
undermined the relationships examined.

\subsection{Appendix C. Calibration of accessibility
parameters}\label{appendix-c.-calibration-of-accessibility-parameters}

This study fitted a built-for-purpose gravity model to derive the
spatial decay parameter \(\beta\). This analysis used data from the 2017
Travel Survey (INEGI 2018). The selected data includes commuting
journeys on weekdays completed by public transport, walking, or a
combination of these. This resulted in a sample including 37,636
observations. The spatial resolution of the journey is the traffic
analysis zone, which includes 111 units.

Alternative specifications were estimated within a doubly-constrained
gravity model, expressed as:

\begin{equation}
  T_{jk} = A_j O_j B_k D_k f(c_{jk})
  \label{eq:gravity-model}
\end{equation}

where \begin{equation} 
  A_j = \left[ \sum{B_k D_k f(c_{jk})} \right]^{-1}
\end{equation}

and \begin{equation} 
  B_k = \left[ \sum{A_j O_j f(c_{jk}) }  \right] ^{-1}
\end{equation}

with balancing factors \(A_j\) and \(B_k\) estimated iteratively.
\(T_{jk}\) are commuter flows between origins (\(j\)) and destinations
(\(k\)), with constraints on the number of journeys at each, i.e.,
\(O_j\) and \(D_k\). \(f(c_{jk})\) is a spatial deterrence or decay
function. The inverse power (\(c_{jk}^{-\beta}\)) and negative
exponential (\(e^{(-\beta c_{jk})}\)) decay functions were tested.
\(\beta\) represents the deterrence parameter. \(c_{jk}\) is the
observed travel time.

A modified specification aimed at capturing an start-up cost for
inter-zone journeys or analogously a benefit of residing and working
within the same zone is also considered (Thorsen and Gitlesen 1998).
This includes a parameter \(\mu\) which affects only the diagonal
elements of the OD matrix. The term is given by the following
expression:

\begin{equation} 
  \mu \delta_{jk}
\end{equation}

where Kronecker delta takes the following value:

\begin{equation}
  \delta_{ij} \left\{
    \begin{array}{ll}
        0 & \quad \text{if   } j \neq k \\
        1 & \quad \text{if   } j = k \\
    \end{array} 
   \right.\\
\end{equation}

The simultaneous calibration of the model parameters \(\beta\) and
\(\mu\) is achieved via optimization by minimising the standardised root
mean square error (SRMSE) using the \emph{Nelder-Mead's simplex
algorithm} as implemented in base \texttt{R} (R Core Team 2021).

The analyses shows that the negative exponential function provided
better fitting estimates compared to the inverse power. Also, the use of
the within-zone parameter \(\mu\) improves the model fit. Accordingly,
the preferred decay function used in the accessibility measures in this
study is the modified negative exponential including \(\mu\) expressed
as follows:

\begin{equation} 
  f(c_{jkt}) = e^{(-\beta c^{PT}_{jkt} + \mu \delta_{jk})}
\label{eq:seleected-decaying-function}
\end{equation}

where, \(c^{PT}_{jkt}\) is the modelled travel time from \(j\) to \(k\)
at the MPTN temporal stage \(t\). The optimized parameters used are
\(\beta = 0.044\) and \(\mu = 1.654\). Figure \ref{fig:impedance-curve}
illustrates the calibrated parameter over the travel time considered in
this study.

  \caption{Impedance curve using the calibrated beta parameter.}
  \label{fig:impedance-curve}
\end{figure}
